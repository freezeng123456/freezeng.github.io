## 3.5.2 Iterative ParaDiag methods (ParaDiag-II)

### From a multistep formula to a circulant preconditioner

ParaDiag-I is difficult to extend directly to high-order integrators such as multistage Runge–Kutta methods. ParaDiag-II instead approximates the time-stepping matrices and solves the all-at-once system in a stationary or Krylov iteration. McDonald et al. (2018) started from the discrete system; Gander and Wu (2019), around the same time, started from continuous waveform relaxation. The two formulations are equivalent after discretization.

First discretize (2.1) with an $m$-step linear multistep method:

$$
\sum_{l=0}^{m}a_l\boldsymbol u_{n-l}
=\Delta t\sum_{l=0}^{m}b_lA\boldsymbol u_{n-l}
+\bar{\boldsymbol g}_n,
\qquad n=1,\ldots,N_t.
$$

The initial history $\{\boldsymbol u_{-(m-1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol u_0\}$ is prescribed. With

$$
\boldsymbol U=(\boldsymbol u_1^\top,\ldots,\boldsymbol u_{N_t}^\top)^\top,
\qquad
K=B_1\otimes I_x-B_2\otimes(\Delta t A),
$$

$B_1$ and $B_2$ are lower triangular banded Toeplitz matrices generated by $\{a_l\}$ and $\{b_l\}$. Replacing both by Strang-type circulants $C_1,C_2$ gives

$$
P=C_1\otimes I_x-C_2\otimes(\Delta t A).
$$

When $P$ is already a sufficiently close approximation to $K$, one can use the stationary iteration directly:

$$
P\Delta\boldsymbol U^k=\boldsymbol r^k
:=\boldsymbol b-K\boldsymbol U^k,
\qquad
\boldsymbol U^{k+1}=\boldsymbol U^k+\Delta\boldsymbol U^k,
\qquad k=0,1,\ldots. \tag{3.49}
$$

Its asymptotic rate is governed by $\rho(P^{-1}K)$. Even when the spectral radius is not small, preconditioned GMRES can still be fast if the spectrum of $P^{-1}K$ is highly clustered.

> [!tip] Insight: stationary iteration watches the spectral radius, Krylov watches spectral clustering
> The error of the stationary iteration (3.49) decays as powers of $\mathcal M=I-P^{-1}K$, so the $k$th error norm is roughly $\rho(\mathcal M)^k$ (when $\mathcal M$ is diagonalizable with a moderately conditioned eigenvector matrix). Hence as soon as $\rho(\mathcal M)=\rho(I-P^{-1}K)\ge1$—that is, whenever $P^{-1}K$ has an eigenvalue far from $1$—stationary iteration stops converging. GMRES behaves entirely differently: its $k$th relative residual satisfies the polynomial-minimization bound
> $$\frac{\|\boldsymbol r^k\|}{\|\boldsymbol r^0\|}\le\kappa(V)\min_{\substack{p\in\Pi_k,\;p(0)=1}}\ \max_{\lambda\in\sigma(P^{-1}K)}|p(\lambda)|,$$
> where $V$ is the eigenvector matrix of $P^{-1}K$ and $\kappa(V)$ is its condition number. This upper bound asks only one thing: whether a low-degree, monic-at-zero polynomial can be made small over the entire spectrum. If the spectrum collapses into a few clusters (especially when the vast majority of eigenvalues equal $1$ exactly), a polynomial whose degree is about the "number of outlying clusters" already forces $\max|p(\lambda)|$ to drop sharply, independent of whether $\rho\ge1$. This is precisely the point of Theorem 3.8 below: even when $\rho(\mathcal M)\ge1$ makes stationary iteration fail, preconditioned GMRES still converges in very few steps as long as the number of non-unit eigenvalues is small.

### Fourier diagonalization and the three-stage solve

Circulant matrices commute and are simultaneously diagonalized by the same discrete Fourier matrix:

$$
C_l=F^*D_lF,\qquad l=1,2,
$$

$$
F=\frac1{\sqrt{N_t}}
\begin{bmatrix}
1&1&\cdots&1\\
1&\omega&\cdots&\omega^{N_t-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
1&\omega^{N_t-1}&\cdots&\omega^{(N_t-1)^2}
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\omega=\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi i}{N_t}\right). \tag{3.50}
$$

Writing $C_l(:,1)$ for the first column,

$$
D_l=\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\sqrt{N_t},F C_l(:,1)\right),
\qquad l=1,2. \tag{3.51}
$$

Thus

$$
P=(F^*\otimes I_x)
\left(D_1\otimes I_x-D_2\otimes(\Delta t A)\right)
(F\otimes I_x),
$$

and one preconditioner application splits into

$$
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol U^a&=(F\otimes I_x)\boldsymbol r^k,\\
(\lambda_{1,n}I_x-\lambda_{2,n}\Delta t A)\boldsymbol u_n^b
&=\boldsymbol u_n^a,
&&n=1,\ldots,N_t,\\
\boldsymbol U&=(F^*\otimes I_x)\boldsymbol U^b.
\end{aligned}
\right. \tag{3.52}
$$

The outer two stages are done by FFTs at cost $O(N_xN_t\log N_t)$; the $N_t$ spatial systems in the middle are mutually independent.

> [!tip] Insight: why circulant matrices can be diagonalized "simultaneously," and why the cost drops to $O(N_xN_t\log N_t)$
> The key is that all circulant matrices share one set of eigenvectors—the columns of the discrete Fourier matrix $F$. Every $N_t\times N_t$ circulant is a polynomial in the cyclic shift operator $S$, and $S=F^*\Omega F$ with $\Omega=\operatorname{diag}(1,\omega,\ldots,\omega^{N_t-1})$. Thus $C_1=p_1(S)=F^*p_1(\Omega)F$ and $C_2=p_2(S)=F^*p_2(\Omega)F$ are diagonalized by the same $F$ and automatically commute, $C_1C_2=C_2C_1$. In the Kronecker structure, the time factors of $P=C_1\otimes I_x-C_2\otimes(\Delta tA)$ are block-diagonalized by the same $F\otimes I_x$, while the spatial matrix $A$ never enters this step—it appears only in the $N_t$ decoupled spatial systems $\lambda_{1,n}I_x-\lambda_{2,n}\Delta tA$. The cost therefore splits into three parts: (step a) and (step c) perform, along the time direction, one length-$N_t$ FFT for each spatial degree of freedom—$N_x$ of them—at cost $O(N_xN_t\log N_t)$; (step b) solves $N_t$ independent spatial systems of size $N_x$, fully in parallel. Compared with solving the temporally dense-coupled all-at-once system $K$ directly, "diagonalizing away" the coupling is exactly where the name ParaDiag (parallel-in-time by diagonalization) comes from. Conversely, if the two time matrices do not commute (for example, a general non-circulant Toeplitz), there is no common eigenvector basis and this simultaneous diagonalization fails at once—which is the fundamental reason a circulant (or $\alpha$-circulant) matrix must be used to approximate the Toeplitz matrix.

### Theorem 3.8: an exact clustering bound

If $A\in\mathbb R^{N_x\times N_x}$ is symmetric negative definite, then $P^{-1}K$ has at most $mN_x$ eigenvalues different from $1$. In exact arithmetic, preconditioned GMRES therefore terminates in at most $mN_x+1$ steps. This bound need not give a small iteration count for large $N_x$, and once $A$ loses symmetry the clustering also weakens.

> [!tip] Insight: where $mN_x$ comes from, why large $N_x$ dilutes it, and why nonsymmetric $A$ destroys it
> The Strang circulant $C_l$ differs from the Toeplitz matrix $B_l$ only along a few "wrap-around" diagonals (the circulant correction entries in the top-right corner): the nonzeros of $C_l-B_l$ are concentrated in the corner and have rank at most $m$ (the bandwidth of the $m$-step formula). Hence
> $$P-K=(C_1-B_1)\otimes I_x-(C_2-B_2)\otimes(\Delta tA)$$
> has rank at most $mN_x$, so $P^{-1}K=I-P^{-1}(P-K)$ is the identity plus a perturbation of rank $\le mN_x$, with at most $mN_x$ eigenvalues departing from $1$. Substituting this into the GMRES polynomial bound of the previous section: take a polynomial that vanishes at these $\le mN_x$ outliers and equals $1$ at $\lambda=1$ (degree $\le mN_x$), which yields termination in at most $mN_x+1$ steps in exact arithmetic. The point is that this bound grows **linearly** with the spatial degrees of freedom $N_x$: refining the mesh raises the worst-case step count proportionally, so it is a qualitative guarantee of "finite-step termination," not a "mesh-independent" fast-convergence guarantee. When $A$ is symmetric negative definite the spatial eigenvalues lie on the negative real axis, the outlying eigenvalues of $P^{-1}K$ are regularly arranged and easily suppressed together by a low-degree polynomial; once $A$ is nonsymmetric (strong advection, waves) the outliers scatter into the complex plane, the clustering loosens, and the actual GMRES step count rises noticeably—precisely the continuous degradation from heat to wave equation in Figure 3.15 below.

### Figure 3.15: spectra and GMRES on three PDE classes

The paper compares the heat equation, advection–diffusion at two viscosities, and the wave equation. All use homogeneous Dirichlet boundaries and $u(x,0)=\sin(2\pi x)$; the wave equation additionally takes $u_t(x,0)=0$. After central differencing in space, the wave equation reads

$$
\boldsymbol u''(t)=A\boldsymbol u(t),
\qquad
\boldsymbol u(0)=\boldsymbol u_0,
\quad \boldsymbol u'(0)=0,
\quad t\in(0,T], \tag{3.53}
$$

where $A=\operatorname{Tri}[1,-2,1]/\Delta x^2$. First-order problems use the trapezoidal rule; the second-order problem uses a parametrized Numerov-type method:

$$
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde{\boldsymbol u}_n-\boldsymbol u_n
+\gamma\Delta t^2A
(\boldsymbol u_{n+1}-2\boldsymbol u_n+\boldsymbol u_{n-1})&=0,\\
\boldsymbol u_{n+1}-2\boldsymbol u_n+\boldsymbol u_{n-1}
-\frac{\Delta t^2A}{12}
(\boldsymbol u_{n+1}+10\widetilde{\boldsymbol u}_n+\boldsymbol u_n)&=0.
\end{aligned}
\right. \tag{3.54}
$$

At $\gamma=0$ this is the classical fourth-order Numerov method, but only conditionally stable; for $\gamma\ge1/120$ it stays fourth order and attains unconditional stability.

> [!tip] Insight: how the parameter $\gamma$ "lifts" conditional stability into unconditional stability, and why stability is a necessary condition for the spectral bound
> The classical Numerov method ($\gamma=0$) is a compact fourth-order scheme, but its interval of absolute stability is finite: for wave-type problems whose eigenvalues sit close to the imaginary axis (close to the negative real axis when $A$ is the Laplacian), $\Delta t$ must be small enough to keep the amplification factor $|r_1^{-1}(z)r_2(z)|$ within bounds. The term $\gamma\Delta t^2A(\boldsymbol u_{n+1}-2\boldsymbol u_n+\boldsymbol u_{n-1})$ in (3.54) acts like a tunable implicit "artificial mass" that balances the high-order terms of $r_1,r_2$: for $\gamma\ge1/120$, all $z=\Delta t^2\lambda\in\mathbb R_-$ satisfy $|r_1^{-1}(z)r_2(z)|\le2$ with equality only at $z=0$ (exactly the stability condition required by the second-order case of Theorem 3.9), giving unconditional stability; and because only an $O(\Delta t^2)$-scale high-order correction is added, the fourth-order accuracy is unaffected. This $1/120$ threshold is taken from Chawla's (1983) stability analysis of Numerov-type methods (supplementary reference). Stability is a **necessary** (not merely sufficient) condition for the spectral bound because the bound of Theorem 3.9 relies on $|r_1^{-1}r_2|\le1$ (first order) or $\le2$ (second order) to hold down the amplification factor of each decoupled subblock; once the scheme loses stability, $|r_1^{-1}r_2|$ exceeds the bound at some $z$ and the eigenvalue of the corresponding subblock escapes the circle of radius $\alpha/(1-\alpha)$—the bottom row of Figure 3.17 with $\gamma=1/120.01$ is a direct visualization of this overshoot.

![Original Figure 3.15: spectra and GMRES residuals after circulant preconditioning for three PDE classes](assets/papers/time-parallelization/source-figures/figure-3-15.svg)

Figure 3.15 uses $T=2$, $\Delta t=1/50$, $\Delta x=1/100$, and $\gamma=1/100$. The left column, panels (a), (c), and (e), plots the spectrum of $P^{-1}K$; the right column, panels (b), (d), and (f), plots the corresponding GMRES residuals; the three rows are the heat, advection–diffusion, and wave equations in turn. In (a) and (b), nearly every heat eigenvalue clusters at $1$ and the residual reaches machine precision in roughly three sweeps. Panels (c) and (d) compare $\nu=10^{-3}$ with $10^{-6}$: the former stays tightly clustered, while the latter's non-unit eigenvalues visibly spread out and need more iterations. The wave spectrum in (e) expands greatly in the imaginary direction, and GMRES in (f) enters a long-tail regime. Together the six panels show that the degradation is continuous: weakening dissipation first destroys the spectral clustering and ultimately makes the circulant preconditioner lose effectiveness in the hyperbolic limit.

### Continuous head–tail coupling and the $\alpha$-circulant matrix

Gander and Wu (2019) introduced, at the continuous level,

$$
\boldsymbol u_t^k(t)=A\boldsymbol u^k(t)+\boldsymbol g(t),
\qquad
\boldsymbol u^k(0)=
\alpha[\boldsymbol u^k(T)-\boldsymbol u^{k-1}(T)]+\boldsymbol u_0, \tag{3.55}
$$

with $\alpha\in\mathbb C$. At convergence, the bracketed difference vanishes and the initial value returns to $\boldsymbol u_0$.

Discretize with a class of one-step formulas:

$$
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
r_1(\Delta tA)\boldsymbol u_n^k
&=r_2(\Delta tA)\boldsymbol u_{n-1}^k+\widetilde{\boldsymbol g}_n,
&&n=1,\ldots,N_t,\\
\boldsymbol u_0^k
&=\alpha(\boldsymbol u_{N_t}^k-\boldsymbol u_{N_t}^{k-1})+\boldsymbol u_0.
\end{aligned}
\right. \tag{3.56}
$$

Backward Euler and the trapezoidal rule correspond, respectively, to

$$
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
r_1&=I_x-\Delta tA,&r_2&=I_x,
&&\text{backward Euler},\\
r_1&=I_x-\tfrac12\Delta tA,&r_2&=I_x+\tfrac12\Delta tA,
&&\text{trapezoidal rule}.
\end{aligned}
\right. \tag{3.57}
$$

Substituting the head–tail condition into the first step, this can be written as

$$
P_\alpha\boldsymbol U^k=\boldsymbol b^k, \tag{3.58a}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol b^k=\boldsymbol b-
\alpha
\begin{bmatrix}
r_2(\Delta tA)\boldsymbol u_{N_t}^{k-1}\\0\\\vdots\\0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\boldsymbol b=
\begin{bmatrix}
r_2(\Delta tA)\boldsymbol u_0+\widetilde{\boldsymbol g}_1\\
\widetilde{\boldsymbol g}_2\\\vdots\\
\widetilde{\boldsymbol g}_{N_t}
\end{bmatrix}. \tag{3.58b}
$$

$$
P_\alpha=
\begin{bmatrix}
r_1& & &-\alpha r_2\\
-r_2&r_1\\
&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&-r_2&r_1
\end{bmatrix}
=I_t\otimes r_1(\Delta tA)-C_\alpha\otimes r_2(\Delta tA), \tag{3.58c}
$$

$$
C_\alpha=
\begin{bmatrix}
0&&&\alpha\\
1&0\\
&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&1&0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

For a Strang-type $\alpha$-circulant matrix,

$$
C_\alpha=V_\alpha D_\alpha V_\alpha^{-1}, \tag{3.59a}
$$

$$
D_\alpha=\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\sqrt{N_t},F\Lambda_\alpha C_\alpha(:,1)\right),
\quad
V_\alpha=\Lambda_\alpha F^*,
\quad
\Lambda_\alpha=\operatorname{diag}
(1,\alpha^{-1/N_t},\ldots,\alpha^{-(N_t-1)/N_t}). \tag{3.59b}
$$

Solving (3.58a) is therefore still a three-stage operation:

$$
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol U^a&=(V_\alpha^{-1}\otimes I_x)\boldsymbol b^k,\\
\left(r_1(\Delta tA)-\lambda_n r_2(\Delta tA)\right)\boldsymbol u_n^b
&=\boldsymbol u_n^a,
&&n=1,\ldots,N_t,\\
\boldsymbol U^k&=(V_\alpha\otimes I_x)\boldsymbol U^b.
\end{aligned}
\right. \tag{3.60}
$$

For $\alpha=1$, $V_\alpha=F^*$ and this algorithm coincides exactly with (3.52). For $\alpha\ne1$, $V_\alpha$ merely multiplies a diagonal scaling before and after the Fourier transform, so FFTs remain usable.

> [!tip] Insight: $\alpha$ just wraps a diagonal scaling around the FFT
> From $V_\alpha=\Lambda_\alpha F^*$: (step a) uses $V_\alpha^{-1}=(\Lambda_\alpha F^*)^{-1}=F\Lambda_\alpha^{-1}$, and (step c) uses $V_\alpha=\Lambda_\alpha F^*$. Written into the Kronecker structure, this is
> $$V_\alpha^{-1}\otimes I_x=(F\otimes I_x)(\Lambda_\alpha^{-1}\otimes I_x),\qquad V_\alpha\otimes I_x=(\Lambda_\alpha\otimes I_x)(F^*\otimes I_x).$$
> That is, the whole transform = a diagonal scaling that multiplies each time point by $\alpha^{\pm n/N_t}$ + one standard FFT/IFFT. At $\alpha=1$, $\Lambda_\alpha=I$, the scaling disappears, and it reduces to the pure FFT diagonalization (3.52) of McDonald et al. (2018); for $0<\alpha<1$ it only adds two $O(N_xN_t)$ diagonal multiplications, leaving the FFT's $O(N_xN_t\log N_t)$ cost and parallel structure completely unchanged. This scaling layer is exactly the source of $\operatorname{Cond}_2(V_\alpha)=1/\alpha$ in the roundoff analysis below—convergence speed and numerical stability are both steered by the same $\alpha$.

![Original Figure 3.16: error decay of head–tail waveform relaxation at two values of alpha](assets/papers/time-parallelization/source-figures/figure-3-16.svg)

Figure 3.16 keeps the PDE settings of Figure 3.15 but switches to periodic boundaries. The left and right panels take $\alpha=0.1$ and $10^{-3}$, respectively, and each panel simultaneously compares the advection–diffusion equation at $\nu=10^{-2}$ and $10^{-6}$ with the wave equation. Here $P$ is singular at $\alpha=1$, so $\alpha<1$ must be taken. With $\alpha=0.1$, all three curves decay linearly and the wave equation is slowest; reduced to $10^{-3}$, all three problem classes reach the neighborhood of $10^{-12}$ in fewer iterations. The figure directly displays $\alpha$'s control of the convergence slope and shows that strongly advective and wave problems converge without Krylov acceleration.

### Discrete equivalence of the two routes

The head–tail discretization can be written as a preconditioned stationary iteration:

$$
P_\alpha\Delta\boldsymbol U^{k-1}
=\boldsymbol r^{k-1}:=\boldsymbol b-K\boldsymbol U^{k-1},
\qquad
\boldsymbol U^k=\boldsymbol U^{k-1}+\Delta\boldsymbol U^{k-1}. \tag{3.61}
$$

Here

$$
K=I_t\otimes r_1(\Delta tA)-B\otimes r_2(\Delta tA), \tag{3.62a}
$$

$$
B=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\1&0\\&\ddots&\ddots\\&&1&0
\end{bmatrix}. \tag{3.62b}
$$

Since $\boldsymbol b^k=(P_\alpha-K)\boldsymbol U^{k-1}+\boldsymbol b$, equation (3.58a) reduces immediately to (3.61). At $\alpha=1$ it is McDonald et al.'s (3.49); for $0<\alpha<1$ it corresponds to the parallel method of Banjai and Peterseim (2012).

> [!tip] Insight: a "who-equals-whom" roadmap
> At the discrete level there is only one object—the all-at-once equation $K\boldsymbol U=\boldsymbol b$ and its block $\alpha$-circulant preconditioner $P_\alpha$—and the two historical routes are just different "entry points" to it:
>
> - **Discrete entry** (McDonald–Pestana–Wathen 2018): replace the Toeplitz time matrix directly by a Strang circulant, corresponding to $\alpha=1$, giving (3.49).
> - **Continuous entry** (Gander & Wu 2019): write head–tail waveform relaxation (3.55) at the continuous level; discretization naturally yields the $\alpha$-circulant $P_\alpha$ with a free parameter $\alpha$ ((3.58)).
>
> Algebraically, $\boldsymbol b^k=(P_\alpha-K)\boldsymbol U^{k-1}+\boldsymbol b$ rewrites the fixed-point iteration (3.58a) into the error–preconditioner form (3.61), and the two are identical. Hence $\alpha=1$ lands on McDonald et al.'s circulant-preconditioned stationary iteration; $0<\alpha<1$ lands on the parallel method of Banjai and Peterseim (2012) (supplementary reference). Under periodic boundaries $P$ is singular at $\alpha=1$ (the zero-frequency row of the circulant degenerates), so $\alpha<1$ must be taken—this is precisely why the continuous entry, by introducing the free parameter $\alpha$, is the more general one, and it explains why Figure 3.16 plots only $\alpha<1$.

The generic one-step formula

$$
r_1(\Delta tA)\boldsymbol u_n
=r_2(\Delta tA)\boldsymbol u_{n-1}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol g}_n,
\qquad n=1,\ldots,N_t, \tag{3.63}
$$

generates the all-at-once equation $K\boldsymbol U=\boldsymbol b$, and $P_\alpha$ is exactly its generalized block $\alpha$-circulant preconditioner.

### Direct discretization of a second-order system

Reformulating a second-order system as first order doubles the storage at each time point. The paper therefore considers the symmetric two-step scheme

$$
r_1(\Delta t^2A)\boldsymbol u_{n+1}
-r_2(\Delta t^2A)\boldsymbol u_n
+r_1(\Delta t^2A)\boldsymbol u_{n-1}
=\widetilde{\boldsymbol g}_n,
\quad n=1,\ldots,N_t-1, \tag{3.64}
$$

and assumes the second initial value $\boldsymbol u_1$ is known. The Numerov-type method corresponds to

$$
r_1(z)=I_x-\frac{z}{12}+\frac{10\gamma z^2}{12},
\qquad
r_2(z)=2I_x+\frac{10z}{12}+\frac{20\gamma z^2}{12},
\quad z=\Delta t^2A.
$$

The all-at-once matrix and preconditioner are

$$
K=\widetilde B\otimes r_1(\Delta t^2A)-B\otimes r_2(\Delta t^2A),
\qquad
P_\alpha=\widetilde C_\alpha\otimes r_1(\Delta t^2A)
-C_\alpha\otimes r_2(\Delta t^2A), \tag{3.65a}
$$

where $B,C_\alpha$ are as above and

$$
\widetilde B=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\0&1\\1&0&1\\&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\&&1&0&1
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\widetilde C_\alpha=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&&\alpha\\0&1&&\alpha\\1&0&1\\&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\&&1&0&1
\end{bmatrix}. \tag{3.65b}
$$

$C_\alpha$ and $\widetilde C_\alpha$ are simultaneously diagonalizable, so the three-stage solve (3.60) also applies to the second-order preconditioner.

### Theorem 3.9: stability, spectral bounds, and rate

For a first-order problem, if (3.63) is stable, that is,

$$
|r_1^{-1}(z)r_2(z)|\le1,
\qquad z\in\sigma(\Delta tA)\subset\mathbb C_-,
$$

the printed paper gives

$$
\frac1{1-\alpha}
\le |\lambda(P_\alpha^{-1}K)|
\le\frac1{1+\alpha},
\qquad 0<\alpha<1. \tag{3.66, as printed}
$$

For a second-order two-step scheme, it claims the same bound when $|r_1^{-1}(z)r_2(z)|\le2$ with equality only at $z=0$.

> [!warning] Check on the printed formula
> For $0<\alpha<1$, the printed left endpoint exceeds the right endpoint, so the interval is empty. Here we preserve the paper's typesetting in full and record the likely correction as $1/(1+\alpha)\le|\lambda|\le1/(1-\alpha)$. The iteration-matrix bound given later is also consistent with "the two denominators swapped." This is a reader's annotation after checking, not a rewriting of the paper's statement.

This general spectral analysis—"valid for any stable one-step or symmetric two-step scheme"—is due to Wu, Zhou and Zhou (2022) (supplementary reference); earlier analyses aimed at specific integrators and specific equation types are scattered across a series of works on parabolic (e.g., Gu and Wu 2020, Lin and Ng 2021) and hyperbolic (e.g., Liu and Wu 2020) problems.

The stationary iteration matrix is

$$
\mathcal M=I-P_\alpha^{-1}K, \tag{3.67}
$$

from which the paper obtains $\rho(\mathcal M)\le\alpha/(1-\alpha)$. A smaller $\alpha$ accelerates convergence, consistent with the experiments in Figure 3.16. Stability of the underlying integrator is a sufficient condition for the spectral bound; the paper's numerical results also indicate it is close to necessary.

![Original Figure 3.17: iteration spectra for the Numerov-type method on the two sides of its stability threshold](assets/papers/time-parallelization/source-figures/figure-3-17.svg)

Figure 3.17 takes $\Delta t=1/16$, $\Delta x=1/128$, $\alpha=0.02$, and compares $T=0.5,10,20$. The top row, at the unconditional-stability threshold $\gamma=1/120$, keeps every eigenvalue inside the dashed circle of radius $\alpha/(1-\alpha)$; the bottom row, at $\gamma=1/120.01$ just below the threshold, loses the spectral bound on long time windows.

> [!tip] Insight: why a threshold difference of only $0.01$ is exposed only on long time windows
> $\gamma=1/120$ sits exactly at Chawla's (1983) unconditional-stability threshold: all $z=\Delta t^2\lambda\in\mathbb R_-$ satisfy $|r_1^{-1}(z)r_2(z)|\le2$ (equality only at $z=0$), the second-order condition of Theorem 3.9 holds, and every eigenvalue of $\mathcal M$ falls inside the circle of radius $\alpha/(1-\alpha)=0.02/0.98\approx0.0204$, and this holds for $T=0.5,10,20$ no matter how long—the spectral bound is independent of window length. $\gamma=1/120.01$ is only slightly smaller, yet it pushes the "stiffest" spatial mode (largest $|z|$, corresponding to the highest frequency) out of the stability region, where $|r_1^{-1}r_2|>2$. Why a large $T$ is needed to see it: with $\Delta t$ fixed, $N_t=T/\Delta t$ grows with $T$, so the $\alpha$-circulant eigenvalues $\lambda_n$ sweep the complex plane more densely and are more likely to "hit" the destabilized subblock, and the eigenvalues it bursts then emerge one by one outside the circle; at $T=0.5$, $N_t$ is too small and may "undersample" the unstable frequency, so the spectral bound appears to still hold. This explains both the top-versus-bottom contrast with $T$ in Figure 3.17 and pins "stability is a necessary condition" onto the figure.

### Roundoff limits at small $\alpha$ and the implementation choice

$\alpha$ cannot be reduced without limit. Since $V_\alpha=\Lambda_\alpha F^*$ and $\operatorname{Cond}_2(F^*)=1$,

$$
\operatorname{Cond}_2(V_\alpha)=\frac1\alpha,
\qquad
\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{ro}}
=O\!\left(\epsilon\operatorname{Cond}_2(V_\alpha)\right)
=O\!\left(\frac\epsilon\alpha\right).
$$

This matrix-factorization error does not necessarily pass in equal measure into the final iterate. Solving directly for $\boldsymbol U^k$ from (3.58) exposes the $O(\epsilon/\alpha)$ amplification; solving first for the error increment $\Delta\boldsymbol U^{k-1}$ from (3.61) and then updating the solution substantially mitigates the roundoff error.

> [!tip] Insight: the two-way trade-off in $\alpha$, and why the correction form "absorbs" the amplification
> The spectral side and the roundoff side demand opposite things of $\alpha$, combining into a U-shaped trade-off:
>
> - **Convergence side**: $\rho(\mathcal M)\le\alpha/(1-\alpha)$; the smaller $\alpha$ is, the more fiercely each stationary-iteration step contracts (Figure 3.16).
> - **Roundoff side**: $\operatorname{Cond}_2(V_\alpha)=1/\alpha$; the diagonal scaling $\Lambda_\alpha$ amplifies the tail components by about $\alpha^{-(N_t-1)/N_t}\approx1/\alpha$, so the floating-point factorization error enters the result as $\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{ro}}=O(\epsilon/\alpha)$.
>
> Hence there exists an intermediate $\alpha$ that minimizes the total error (empirically $\alpha\in[10^{-3},10^{-1}]$). Why the correction form helps: when solving (3.58) directly, the $O(\epsilon/\alpha)$ **absolute** error is added straight onto the full solution $\boldsymbol U^k$ and can no longer be reduced once it approaches that magnitude. Using (3.61) instead, each step solves for the **error increment** $\Delta\boldsymbol U^{k-1}$, whose norm shrinks as the iteration converges; the roundoff amplification acts on an ever-smaller increment, so the resulting absolute perturbation $O(\epsilon\|\Delta\boldsymbol U^{k-1}\|/\alpha)$ shrinks in step, and the final accuracy is no longer locked at $\epsilon/\alpha$. This shares its origin with classical iterative refinement: compute the large-magnitude solution and the small-magnitude correction separately. The left and right panels of Figure 3.18 are exactly a comparison of this mechanism.

![Original Figure 3.18: roundoff error under two implementations and three values of alpha](assets/papers/time-parallelization/source-figures/figure-3-18.svg)

Figure 3.18 simultaneously compares $\alpha=10^{-3},10^{-6},10^{-11}$. The left panel solves directly for $\boldsymbol U^k$: the three curves stall at roughly $10^{-12}$, $10^{-9}$, and $10^{-4}$, and the smaller $\alpha$ is, the earlier the $\epsilon/\alpha$ amplification appears. The right panel solves first for $\Delta\boldsymbol U^{k-1}$ and then updates, and all three parameters finally drop to about $10^{-13}$. This set of panels separates the "spectral convergence of the algorithm" from the "roundoff stability of the implementation"; reducing $\alpha$ turns into realized accuracy only when paired with the correction form. The paper points to Wu, Yang and Zhou (2025) for a more systematic roundoff analysis.

For general multistep methods, (3.66) need not hold. Volterra partial integro-differential equations produce dense lower triangular Toeplitz time matrices; if the quadrature weights are positive and monotone, existing results give $|\lambda(P_\alpha^{-1}K)|=1+O(\alpha)$.

### Nonlinear Newton–Krylov

For $\boldsymbol u'=f(t,\boldsymbol u)$ with backward Euler, first apply Newton's method to the nonlinear all-at-once equation:

$$
J\Delta\boldsymbol U^l=\boldsymbol b-F(\boldsymbol U^l),
\qquad
\boldsymbol U^{l+1}=\boldsymbol U^l+\Delta\boldsymbol U^l. \tag{3.68}
$$

Here

$$
J=B\otimes I_x-\nabla F_l,
\qquad
\nabla F_l=\operatorname{blkdiag}\!\left(
\nabla f(\boldsymbol u_1^l,t_1),\ldots,
\nabla f(\boldsymbol u_{N_t}^l,t_{N_t})
\right),
$$

$$
B=\frac1{\Delta t}
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\-1&1\\&\ddots&\ddots\\&&-1&1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The inner GMRES uses

$$
P_\alpha=C_\alpha\otimes I_x-I_t\otimes A_l,
$$

where $A_l$ is the average of all $\nabla f(\boldsymbol u_n^l,t_n)$. Generally $\rho(P_\alpha^{-1}J)>1$, so the stationary iteration (3.61) is unsuitable for this Jacobian system; the preconditioned spectrum still clusters, which makes GMRES effective. Shortening the time window strengthens the clustering and lowers the inner iteration count, and the nearest Kronecker approximation of Section 3.5.1 can improve the preconditioner further.

> [!tip] Insight: the nonlinear case is the archetype of "$\rho\ge1$ yet GMRES is still fast"
> Here $P_\alpha$ replaces the time-varying $\nabla f(\boldsymbol u_n^l,t_n)$ with a single **averaged** Jacobian $A_l$, and the approximation error is usually large enough to make $\rho(P_\alpha^{-1}J)>1$—the stationary iteration (3.61) diverges outright. But as long as the Jacobian varies mildly across time points, the spectrum of $P_\alpha^{-1}J$ still clusters in a small cloud near $1$, and by the polynomial bound at the top of this page GMRES still converges in a few steps. This echoes the divide between Krylov and stationary iteration: stationary iteration is condemned by $\rho$, whereas Krylov only cares whether the spectrum can be held down by a low-degree polynomial. The shorter the time window, the closer $A_l$ is to the true Jacobian, the tighter the clustering, and the fewer inner steps are needed; the nearest Kronecker approximation of Section 3.5.1 tightens the spectrum further through a low-rank structure that better matches $\nabla F_l$.
