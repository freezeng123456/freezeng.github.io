## 75: an information bottleneck for out-of-distribution behaviour

### What the paper targets

Deterministic network regression and deterministic operator networks give point predictions with no calibrated uncertainty. The standard remedies — Bayesian neural networks with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo posterior sampling, Gaussian processes, deep ensembles — either scale badly with data size or fail to inflate uncertainty away from the training data. The specific target is **extrapolation**: a method should report high uncertainty where the input is far from the training distribution. The authors note that deep ensembles in particular do not increase their spread for out-of-distribution test inputs.

### Three constructions

**An information bottleneck objective with data augmentation.** The base principle encodes an input $X$ into a latent $Z$, decodes $Y$ from $Z$, and maximises $I(Z;Y)-\beta I(Z;X)$. This paper uses instead

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{IB}}=I(Z;Y)-\beta\,I(\tilde Z;\tilde X),
$$

where $\tilde X\sim\tilde p(x)\propto p(x)^{1/\tau}$ with $\tau>1$ is a **flattened** version of the training input distribution generated by a volume-preserving normalizing flow, and $\tilde Z$ is its encoding. **Replacing $I(Z;X)$ by $I(\tilde Z;\tilde X)$ is the decisive change for the out-of-distribution problem**: it forces the latent variable to become approximately independent of an out-of-distribution input.

**A confidence-aware encoder.**

$$
z=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(m(x)\bigr)\,\bar z(x)
+\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\mathbf 1-m(x)\bigr)\,z_0,
\qquad z_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I),
$$

where $\bar z(x)$ is a deterministic feature vector and each component of $m(x)\in[0,1]^{\dim z}$ is a per-feature confidence. $m(x)=\mathbf 1$ means "$x$ resembles training data, predict deterministically"; $m(x)=\mathbf 0$ means "$x$ is out of distribution, $z$ collapses to pure noise".

**The variational bound actually optimised.** With a Gaussian decoder $y\mid z\sim\mathcal N(\mu_D(z),\Sigma_D(z))$ and diagonal $\Sigma_D$,

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{VIB}}
=\mathbb E\bigl[\log q_D(y\mid z)\bigr]
-\beta\,\mathbb E\Bigl[\log\frac{q_E(\tilde z\mid\tilde x)}{e(\tilde z)}\Bigr]
\ \le\ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{IB}}-\mathcal H(Y),
$$

where $e(\tilde z)$ is a **normalizing flow** model of the marginal of $\tilde Z$ and $\mathcal H(Y)$ is the model-independent entropy of $Y$.

The confidence gate has one implementation detail worth recording: $\log m(x)$ comes from a network with LogSigmoid output and is then re-gated so it decays toward zero in regions of low generative-model density, using the first and fifth percentiles of $\tilde p$ as thresholds. **That step is the mechanism that drives $m(x)$ down to around $10^{-6}$ for very-low-density inputs**, and therefore the actual source of the out-of-distribution behaviour.

### The operator-learning version

The DeepONet baseline is $\mathcal F_\theta(u)(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_i(o)t_i(y)$ with $o=\{u(x_j)\}_{j=1}^m$ the sensor readings. The bottleneck is placed **in the branch network**:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{IBONet}}=I\bigl((Y,Z);s(Y)\bigr)-\beta\,I(\tilde Z;\tilde O),
$$

with the same encoder, and a decoder that makes DeepONet output **both** the mean and the log standard deviation:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\mu_D(y,z)\\ \log\sigma_D(y,z)\end{pmatrix}
=\sum_{i=1}^{n} b_i(z)\,t_i(y),
\qquad
\Sigma_D(y,z)=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\sigma_D(y,z)\bigr)^2 .
$$

The experiments cover one-dimensional discontinuous regression (against a Gaussian process, a Hamiltonian Monte Carlo Bayesian network and a deep ensemble), operator learning for a diffusion-reaction equation and an advection equation, California housing prices as real tabular data, and a large-scale climate model. The reported comparison is that, like the Gaussian process and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and unlike the deep ensemble, this method produces larger standard deviations in data-poor regions.

## 95: sensor count and position may vary

### The problem

DeepONet requires the sensor locations $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^m$ encoding the input function to be **identical in number and position across every training and test sample**, which is unrealistic for experimental or irregularly simulated data. Variable-input operator networks fix the first limitation by making the branch network permutation-invariant with a lightweight transformer, but they still assume the observations are dense and informative enough to determine a **deterministic** map from input to output. When sensors are sparse, irregular or noisy, or when the operator itself is random (an SDE), the output is genuinely uncertain and no deterministic surrogate can be correct. The stated gap is that few studies explicitly treat uncertainty quantification in permutation-invariant operator learning.

### The stochastic operator model

$$
\hat{\mathcal G}\bigl(h(\mathcal O),z\bigr)(y)=\sum_{n=1}^{p}b^{n}\bigl(h(\mathcal O),z\bigr)\,t^{n}(y),
$$

where $h(\mathcal O)$ is a **set transformer** giving a permutation-invariant, fixed-dimensional representation of a variable-size observation set $\mathcal O$, and $z$ is a latent random variable with a simple standard-Gaussian prior. **The latent $z$ carries both kinds of uncertainty**: the epistemic uncertainty from incomplete observation and the aleatoric randomness intrinsic to the operator.

The set transformer works in two steps. The input embedding embeds coordinate and value separately and **sums** them rather than concatenating:

$$
\Lambda_{i}=\Lambda_{x}(x_{i})+\Lambda_{\kappa}\bigl(\kappa(x_{i})\bigr)\in\mathbb R^{d_{\mathrm{emb}}} .
$$

Attention pooling uses $H$ heads, each with a trainable attention-weight network $w_l$ and value-projection network $v_l$:

$$
h^{(l)}(\mathcal O)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\frac{\exp\bigl(w_{l}(\Lambda_{i})/\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{emb}}}\bigr)}
{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\exp\bigl(w_{l}(\Lambda_{k})/\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{emb}}}\bigr)}\,
v_{l}(\Lambda_{i}),
\qquad
h(\mathcal O)=\bigl[h^{(1)},\dots,h^{(H)}\bigr]^{\top} .
$$

**The softmax-normalised sum over $i$ is exactly what makes the map both order-invariant and size-agnostic.**

### One deliberate scaling choice

The decoder takes grid values $\bar u=(u(y_1),\dots,u(y_M))^{\top}$ with

$$
p_{D}(\bar u\mid\mathcal O,z)=\prod_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal N
\Bigl(u(y_{i})\Bigm| \hat{\mathcal G}(h(\mathcal O),z)(y_{i}),\ M\sigma_{u}^{2}\Bigr),
$$

and note that the artificial noise variance is multiplied by $M$. That is **deliberate**: only then does the discrete loss converge, as $M\to\infty$, to a conditional variational-autoencoder loss defined in **function space** instead of degenerating. The appendix makes this precise: for uniform grids and large $M$, $\sum_i(\cdot)^2/M\approx\|\cdot\|_{L^2}^2/|\Omega_u|$, so choosing $\sigma=\sqrt{|\Omega_u|}\,\sigma_u$ makes the discrete loss a discretisation of the functional loss.

The training loss is the negative evidence lower bound in closed form,

$$
\mathcal L=\tfrac12\mathbb E\Bigl[-\log|\Sigma_{z}|+\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma_{z}+\|\mu_{z}\|^{2}-d_{z}\Bigr]
+\frac{1}{2\sigma_{u}^{2}}\mathbb E\Bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{M}
\frac{\bigl(\hat{\mathcal G}(h(\mathcal O),z)(y_{i})-u(y_{i})\bigr)^{2}}{M}\Bigr],
$$

whose first bracket is the analytic Gaussian-to-standard-Gaussian KL and whose second is the reconstruction term. Homogeneous Dirichlet conditions are hard-constrained by the architecture: $u(x)=(1-x)(1+x)u_\theta(x)$ on $[-1,1]$ in one dimension and $u(x,y)=x(1-x)y(1-y)u_\theta(x,y)$ on $[0,1]^2$ in two.

### Making the uncertainty claims falsifiable

Evaluation uses the Wasserstein-2 distance together with relative errors of the mean and standard deviation. **The reference distributions are not Monte Carlo over the trained model but exact Gaussian process conditionals**: since $\log\kappa\sim\mathcal{GP}(\mu,\mathcal K)$, the posterior given sensor values remains a Gaussian process with

$$
\mu_{\text{post}}(x)=\mu(x)+\mathcal K(x,x_{\text{obs}})
\mathcal K(x_{\text{obs}},x_{\text{obs}})^{-1}(y_{\text{obs}}-\mu(x_{\text{obs}})),
$$

$$
\mathcal K_{\text{post}}(x,x')=\mathcal K(x,x')-\mathcal K(x,x_{\text{obs}})
\mathcal K(x_{\text{obs}},x_{\text{obs}})^{-1}\mathcal K(x_{\text{obs}},x') ,
$$

with the inverses becoming $[\mathcal K+\sigma^2I]^{-1}$ under additive noise. One thousand posterior samples per observation set are drawn and pushed through a finite-difference solver to build the reference conditional distribution. **That step is what makes the uncertainty claims falsifiable rather than self-referential**, and it is worth borrowing as a methodological practice.

At inference $z$ is sampled from the **prior** rather than the encoder, since $\bar u$ is unknown at test time.

## 98: replacing the noise term with a Gaussian process prior

Paper 98 follows the confidence-aware encoder idea of paper 75 with two substitutions: the noise term $z_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ becomes a **Gaussian process prior**, and the mean of the Gaussian decoder becomes a **neural operator**. The point of the first substitution is uncertainty with **correlation structure**: independent Gaussian noise gives pointwise uncertainty, whereas a Gaussian process prior correlates the uncertainty across locations, which is closer to the behaviour of a PDE solution.

## 107: changing the learned object from initial-condition dependence to the transition density

### The problem

In ensemble forecasting and data assimilation the stochastic dynamics is fixed while the **initial distribution changes** with each scenario or posterior update, so a classical Fokker-Planck solver or a physics-informed solver must be re-run from scratch every time. Casting this as operator learning (initial distribution to time-dependent solution) is natural, but the paper argues that applying DeepONet, a Fourier neural operator or Green's-function operator learning directly to Fokker-Planck equations "requires additional treatments of boundary conditions and PDF constraints", namely non-negativity and unit mass.

### The core move: learn the transition density

The alternative is to learn the **transition density** instead of the initial-condition dependence:

$$
\frac{\partial p(\bm x,t\mid\bm x_{0})}{\partial t}
=\mathcal L^{*}_{\bm f,\bm g}\,p(\bm x,t\mid\bm x_{0}),
\qquad
p(\bm x,0\mid\bm x_{0})=\delta(\bm x-\bm x_{0}),
$$

and then recover any initial condition through the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation

$$
p(\bm x,t)=\int_{\Omega_{0}}p(\bm x,t\mid\bm x_{0})\,p_{0}(\bm x_{0})\,\mathrm d\bm x_{0} .
$$

**This is the paper's core move: the learned object is independent of $p_0$, so new initial data need no retraining at all.** The price is that the transition density solves an equation with a **Dirac initial condition**, which is singular and cannot be reached from a smooth base distribution by any invertible map.

### A conditional flow with a linearised base process

The density model is a conditional normalizing flow in which both the transformation $T$ and the base law depend on $(\bm x_0,t)$:

$$
p_{\bm X_{t}\mid\bm X_{0}=\bm x_{0}}(\bm x)
=p_{\bm Z_{t}(\bm x_{0})}\bigl(T(\bm x;\theta(\bm x_{0},t))\bigr)\,
\bigl|\det\nabla_{\bm x}T(\bm x;\theta(\bm x_{0},t))\bigr| .
$$

Because it is a flow, non-negativity and unit mass hold **by construction** — the paper's answer to the objection about probability-density constraints, matching the stance of the [[en/computational-mathematics/paper-notes/scientific-machine-learning/normalizing-flows-for-densities|density-flow page]].

The Dirac singularity is handled by giving the base process a **linearised stochastic differential equation**, first-order Taylor on the drift and zero-order on the diffusion, both at $\bm x_0$:

$$
\mathrm d\widehat{\bm X}_{t}
=\bigl(\bm f(\bm x_{0})+\nabla\bm f(\bm x_{0})(\widehat{\bm X}_{t}-\bm x_{0})\bigr)\mathrm dt
+\bm g(\bm x_{0})\mathrm d\bm W_{t},
\qquad \widehat{\bm X}_{0}=\bm x_{0},
$$

which is a Gaussian process with closed-form mean and covariance:

$$
\mathbb E\bigl[\widehat{\bm X}_{t}\bigr]
=\bm x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\nabla\bm f(\bm x_{0})(t-s)}\bm f(\bm x_{0})\,\mathrm ds,
$$

$$
\bm\Sigma\bigl[\widehat{\bm X}_{t}\bigr]
=\int_{0}^{t}e^{\nabla\bm f(\bm x_{0})(t-s)}\bm g(\bm x_{0})\bm g(\bm x_{0})^{\top}
e^{\nabla\bm f(\bm x_{0})^{\top}(t-s)}\,\mathrm ds .
$$

**As $t\to0$ this base process degenerates automatically to a Gaussian centred at $\bm x_0$ with vanishing covariance**, so the flow never has to bridge from a smooth distribution to a Dirac: the singularity is absorbed by the base law itself. It is a clean example of aligning the architecture with the analytic structure of the solution.

## Side-by-side comparison

| No. | Source of uncertainty                    | Representation                              | Who guarantees the constraints        |
| --- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| 75  | inputs falling out of distribution       | confidence gate plus Gaussian decoder       | nobody (soft constraints)             |
| 95  | sparse observations or a random operator | latent variable plus set transformer        | architecture, for boundary conditions |
| 98  | as in 75, with correlation structure     | Gaussian process prior plus neural operator | pending                               |
| 107 | not applicable (varying initial data)    | conditional flow plus linearised base       | the flow, for density constraints     |

One judgement runs through all four: **"a prediction should be a distribution" can be located in three different places in an operator-learning architecture.** Paper 75 puts it in the encoder's confidence gate, paper 95 in a latent variable, paper 98 in the correlation structure of the prior. Paper 107 makes a different point: sometimes the right move is not to add uncertainty to the prediction but to change to a learned object that does not depend on the varying parameter at all.
