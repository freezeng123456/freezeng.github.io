## 75: an information bottleneck for out-of-distribution behaviour

### The idea

A deterministic network's output near the training data and far from it look identical: a string of numbers, with no flag saying "I do not know". Deep ensembles fail to supply that flag because every member saw the same data, so in the extrapolation region they tend to agree **on the same wrong answer** — and ensemble spread therefore shrinks exactly where it should grow.

This paper installs a **gate** in the middle of the network. The input is first encoded into a latent $z$, and $y$ is decoded from $z$; a gate $m(x)$ decides how much of $z$ comes from the input and how much from pure noise. Where $x$ sits in a dense region of training data the gate is open, $z$ is fully determined by $x$, and the prediction is sharp. Where $x$ sits somewhere the training data never reached, the gate closes, $z$ degenerates into standard Gaussian noise, and the decoder can only return a wide distribution. **"I do not know" is therefore not estimated after the fact; it is behaviour the architecture forces in low-density regions.**

That leaves the question of how to train the gate: there are no out-of-distribution samples in the training set, so the gate has no reason to learn to close. The paper supplies that reason by rewriting the information-bottleneck objective — the compression term is changed from "compress the training inputs" to "compress a **flattened** input distribution". The flattened distribution has wider support, so during training the gate is forced to practise closing on the fringes of the training distribution.

### Setting

The base information bottleneck encodes an input $X$ into a latent $Z$, decodes $Y$ from $Z$, and maximises

$$
I(Z;Y)-\beta\,I(Z;X),
$$

with $I(\cdot;\cdot)$ mutual information and $\beta\in[0,1]$ trading prediction against compression. The paper's specific target is **extrapolation**: a UQ method should report high uncertainty where the input is far from the training distribution. The standard remedies (Bayesian neural networks with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo posterior sampling, Gaussian processes, deep ensembles) either scale badly with data size or fail to inflate uncertainty away from the training data; the authors note in particular that deep ensembles do not increase their spread for out-of-distribution test inputs.

### Derivation

**Step one: replace the compression term by compression on a flattened distribution.** The objective (their Eq. 6) is

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{IB}}=I(Z;Y)-\beta\,I(\tilde Z;\tilde X),
$$

where $\tilde X\sim\tilde p(x)\propto p(x)^{1/\tau}$ with $\tau>1$ is a **flattened** version of the training input distribution, generated by a volume-preserving normalizing flow (a general incompressible-flow network, GIN), and $\tilde Z$ is its encoding. **Replacing $I(Z;X)$ by $I(\tilde Z;\tilde X)$ is the fix for the out-of-distribution problem**: flattening pushes mass towards the fringe of the training distribution, and the compression term penalises the dependence of $\tilde Z$ on $\tilde X$ there, so the latent becomes approximately independent of out-of-distribution inputs.

**Step two: the confidence-aware encoder.** The encoder (their Eq. 7) is a componentwise interpolation,

$$
z=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(m(x)\bigr)\,\bar z(x)
+\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\mathbf 1-m(x)\bigr)\,z_0,
\qquad z_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I),
$$

where $\bar z(x)$ is a deterministic feature vector and each component of $m(x)\in[0,1]^{\dim z}$ is a per-feature confidence. $m(x)=\mathbf 1$ means "$x$ resembles training data, predict deterministically"; $m(x)=\mathbf 0$ means "$x$ is out of distribution, $z$ collapses to pure noise".

**Step three: Gaussian decoder and the variational bound.** The decoder takes $y\mid z\sim\mathcal N(\mu_D(z),\Sigma_D(z))$ with $\Sigma_D$ diagonal; $\mu_D$ and $\log\operatorname{diag}\Sigma_D$ are both MLP outputs. What is actually optimised (their Eqs. 8–9) is

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{VIB}}
=\mathbb E_{(x,y,z)\sim p(x,y)q_E(z\mid x)}\bigl[\log q_D(y\mid z)\bigr]
-\beta\,\mathbb E_{(\tilde x,\tilde z)\sim\tilde p(\tilde x)q_E(\tilde z\mid\tilde x)}
\Bigl[\log\frac{q_E(\tilde z\mid\tilde x)}{e(\tilde z)}\Bigr]
\ \le\ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{IB}}-\mathcal H(Y),
$$

where $e(\tilde z)$ is a **normalizing-flow** model of the marginal of $\tilde Z$ and $\mathcal H(Y)$ is the entropy of $Y$. Because $\mathcal H(Y)$ is model-independent, maximising $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{VIB}}$ raises a lower bound on $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{IB}}$. Equality holds when $(q_E,q_D,e)$ are perfectly trained and $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{VIB}}$ is maximised.

The mini-batch estimators (Supplementary S4) are

$$
\widehat{I(Z;Y)}\approx\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}\log q_D(y_b\mid z_b),
\qquad
\widehat{I(\tilde Z;\tilde X)}\approx\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\bigl[\log q_E(\tilde z_b\mid\tilde x_b)-\log e(\tilde z_b)\bigr].
$$

**Step four: the mechanism that actually shuts the gate.** One implementation detail is worth recording. $\log m(x)$ is produced by a network with a LogSigmoid output and then re-gated so that it decays towards zero wherever the generative model's density is low:

$$
\log m(x)^{\mathrm{new}}=\mathrm{LogSigmoid}\!\left(
\log\frac{m(x)^-}{1-m(x)^-}
+\log\!\left(\tanh\!\left(
\frac{\mathrm{relu}\bigl(\log\tilde p(x)-\log\tilde p'\bigr)}
{\log\tilde p''-\log\tilde p'}\right)+10^{-12}\right)\right),
$$

with $m(x)^-=\min\{m(x),\mathrm{Sigmoid}(10^6)\}$ and $\tilde p'$, $\tilde p''$ the 1st and 5th percentiles of the GIN density $p_G(x)$. Once $\log\tilde p(x)$ falls below the first percentile the relu vanishes, the $\tanh$ term drops to $10^{-12}$, and $\log m$ is driven towards $-\infty$. **This is the mechanism that pushes $m(x)$ down to around $10^{-6}$ for very-low-density inputs**, and hence the real source of the out-of-distribution behaviour.

### The operator-learning version

The DeepONet baseline is $\mathcal F_\theta(u)(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_i(o)t_i(y)$ with $o=\{u(x_j)\}_{j=1}^m$ the sensor readings, trained on

$$
\mathcal L(\theta)=\frac{1}{NP}\sum_{l}\sum_{j}
\bigl|\mathcal F_\theta(u^l)(y^l_j)-s^l(y^l_j)\bigr|^2 .
$$

The bottleneck is placed **in the branch network**:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{IBONet}}=I\bigl((Y,Z);s(Y)\bigr)-\beta\,I(\tilde Z;\tilde O),
$$

with the same encoder acting on sensor readings, $z(o)=\operatorname{diag}(m(o))\bar z(o)+\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf 1-m(o))z_0$. The decoder makes the DeepONet output the mean **and** the log standard deviation at once:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\mu_D(y,z)\\ \log\sigma_D(y,z)\end{pmatrix}
=\sum_{i=1}^{n} b_i(z)\,t_i(y),
\qquad
\Sigma_D(y,z)=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\sigma_D(y,z)\bigr)^2 ,
$$

with the corresponding bound $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{VIBONet}}\le\mathcal L_{\mathrm{IBONet}}-\mathcal H(s(Y))$.

> [!warning] A misprinted normalising constant
> The normalising constant above is printed as $NP$ while the inner sum runs to $M$. Only one of the two can be right dimensionally; this is a typo in the paper.

The training algorithm (Supplementary S4, Algorithm 1) runs as follows: fit the GIN to $\{x_1,\dots,x_N\}$ to obtain $\tilde p$; at each step draw a mini-batch and optionally apply mixup (draw a random permutation $(I_1,\dots,I_B)$ and $\lambda_b\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha)$, set $x_b:=\lambda_bx_b+(1-\lambda_b)x_{I_b}$ and likewise for $y_b$); draw $\tilde x_1,\dots,\tilde x_B$ from the GIN; build $z_b$ and $\tilde z_b$ through the confidence gate; estimate $\widehat{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{VIB}}}$ and take a gradient **ascent** step on all parameters of $q_D$, $q_E$ and $e$. At prediction time, repeatedly draw $(Y,Z)\sim q_E(z\mid x)q_D(y\mid z)$ for a new $x$ and report the sample mean and variance, i.e. Monte Carlo evaluation of $\hat p(y\mid x)=\int q_E(z\mid x)q_D(y\mid z)\,\mathrm dz$.

### Theorems

**There is no convergence theorem.** The theoretical content is in two places: the derivation of the variational lower bound in Supplementary S1, and the analysis in S5 of why GIN data augmentation makes the latent approximately independent of out-of-distribution inputs. No error rate or consistency result is given.

What it does not establish is calibration quality: no coverage, CRPS or interval score is reported, and "larger standard deviations" remains a qualitative comparison against the reference bands.

### Relation to the others

This is the first of the group's three latent-variable UQ papers. Paper 98 reuses the **same confidence-aware encoder** but replaces the noise term $z_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ with a **Gaussian-process prior** and the Gaussian decoder's mean with a neural operator; paper 95 keeps the conditional-VAE/ELBO machinery but swaps the confidence gate for a permutation-invariant set-transformer encoder. The normalizing-flow marginal model $e(\tilde z)$ connects this page to the [[en/computational-mathematics/paper-notes/scientific-machine-learning/normalizing-flows-for-densities|density-flow line]], and GIN is itself a volume-preserving normalizing flow.

## 95: the number and position of sensors may vary

### The idea

A DeepONet branch network consumes a **fixed-length vector**: the first entry is the first sensor's reading, the second entry the second sensor's, and so on. Drop a sensor or move one, and the network is unusable. Experimental data and irregularly-simulated data are almost always like that.

The fix is to change the branch input from a vector to a **set**. Each sensor contributes a pair $(x_i,\kappa(x_i))$; the network embeds every pair into a common feature space and then aggregates them by a softmax-weighted sum into one fixed-length vector. The sum runs over set elements, so order does not matter; the softmax denominator runs over the same set, so the count does not matter either.

That alone is not enough. When sensors are sparse the same readings are consistent with many different underlying coefficient curves and hence with many different solutions — **the input-to-output map is not a function at all, it is a conditional distribution**. A deterministic surrogate is wrong in principle; the best it can do is learn the conditional mean. So the paper adds a latent variable $z$: given the observation set, different $z$ give different candidate solutions, and the law of $z$ is the predictive distribution. That single $z$ carries two things at once — the **epistemic** uncertainty of incomplete observation, and the **aleatoric** randomness of an operator that is itself stochastic (the coefficient is a random field, say, or the right-hand side is a second unobserved random source).

### Setting

DeepONet requires the sensor locations $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^m$ encoding the input function to be **identical in number and position across every training and test sample**. The Variable-Input Deep Operator Network (VIDON) fixes the first limitation by making the branch permutation-invariant with a lightweight transformer, but still assumes the observations are dense and informative enough to determine a **deterministic** input-to-output map. The paper's stated gap is that few studies have explicitly investigated uncertainty quantification within permutation-invariant operator-learning frameworks. It also records a secondary limitation of prior work: the original DeepONet handled stochastic operators by a Karhunen–Loève expansion, which restricted it to **low-dimensional** random inputs.

The baseline DeepONet (their Eq. 2.1) is

$$
\hat{\mathcal G}(\kappa)(y)=\sum_{n=1}^{p}
\underbrace{b^{n}(\mathcal O)}_{\text{branch}}\,
\underbrace{t^{n}(y)}_{\text{trunk}}
=\sum_{n=1}^{p}b^{n}\bigl(\kappa(x_1),\dots,\kappa(x_m)\bigr)\,t^{n}(y),
$$

with $\mathcal O=\{(x_i,\kappa(x_i))\}_{i=1}^m$ the observation set and $p$ the truncation rank. The central object of this paper (their Eq. 2.2) is

$$
\hat{\mathcal G}\bigl(h(\mathcal O),z\bigr)(y)=\sum_{n=1}^{p}b^{n}\bigl(h(\mathcal O),z\bigr)\,t^{n}(y),
$$

where $h(\mathcal O)$ is a **set transformer** giving a permutation-invariant, fixed-dimensional representation of a variable-size $\mathcal O$, and $z$ is a latent random variable with a simple prior (standard Gaussian).

### Derivation

**The set transformer** works in two steps. The input embedding (their Eq. 3.1) embeds coordinate and value by separate networks and **sums** them (rather than concatenating):

$$
\Lambda_{i}=\Lambda_{x}(x_{i})+\Lambda_{\kappa}\bigl(\kappa(x_{i})\bigr)\in\mathbb R^{d_{\mathrm{emb}}},
\qquad i=1,\dots,m .
$$

Attention pooling (their Eqs. 3.2–3.3) uses $H$ heads, each with a trainable attention-weight network $w_l$ and value-projection network $v_l$:

$$
h^{(l)}(\mathcal O)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\underbrace{\frac{\exp\bigl(w_{l}(\Lambda_{i})/\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{emb}}}\bigr)}
{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\exp\bigl(w_{l}(\Lambda_{k})/\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{emb}}}\bigr)}}_{\text{attention weights}}\,
\underbrace{v_{l}(\Lambda_{i})}_{\text{value projection}},
\qquad
h(\mathcal O)=\bigl[h^{(1)},\dots,h^{(H)}\bigr]^{\top} .
$$

**The softmax-normalised sum over $i$ is exactly what delivers both order invariance and size independence**: numerator and denominator run over the same index set, so permuting $\{1,\dots,m\}$ leaves the result unchanged, and the structure stays well defined as $m$ varies. The module is stated to follow the VIDON architecture.

**Decoder, and one deliberate piece of scaling.** The solution is represented by grid values $\bar u=(u(y_1),\dots,u(y_M))^{\top}$, and the decoder (their Eq. 3.4) is

$$
p_{D}(\bar u\mid\mathcal O,z)=\prod_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal N
\Bigl(u(y_{i})\Bigm| \hat{\mathcal G}(h(\mathcal O),z)(y_{i}),\ M\sigma_{u}^{2}\Bigr),
$$

with prior $p(z)=\mathcal N(z\mid0,I)$ and $\sigma_u^2$ a small positive hyperparameter ("artificial noise"). Note that the artificial noise variance is multiplied by $M$. This is **deliberate** (their Remark 3.1): only then does the discrete loss converge, as $M\to\infty$, to a conditional variational autoencoder loss defined on **function space**, instead of degenerating. Appendix A.3 makes it precise: for uniform grids and large $M$, $\sum_i(\cdot)^2/M\approx\|\cdot\|_{L^2}^2/|\Omega_u|$, so choosing $\sigma=\sqrt{|\Omega_u|}\,\sigma_u$ makes the discrete loss a discretisation of the functional loss $\mathcal L_f$.

**Encoder and ELBO.** The variational posterior is

$$
q_{E}(z\mid\mathcal O,\bar u)=\mathcal N
\Bigl(z\Bigm|\mu_{z}\bigl(h(\mathcal O),\bar u\bigr),\ \Sigma_{z}\bigl(h(\mathcal O),\bar u\bigr)\Bigr),
$$

with $\Sigma_z$ **diagonal** and the encoder reusing the _same_ embedding $h(\mathcal O)$. Applying Jensen's inequality to the marginal likelihood,

$$
\log p_{D}(\bar u\mid\mathcal O)=\log\!\int p_{D}(\bar u\mid\mathcal O,z)p(z)\,\mathrm dz
\ \ge\ -D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q_{E}(z\mid\bar u,\mathcal O)\,\|\,p(z)\bigr)
+\mathbb E_{q_{E}}\bigl[\log p_{D}(\bar u\mid\mathcal O,z)\bigr],
$$

with equality iff $q_E$ equals the true posterior $p_D(z\mid\bar u,\mathcal O)$. The training loss is the negative ELBO, which is closed-form because prior and posterior are both Gaussian (their Eq. 3.5):

$$
\mathcal L=\tfrac12\mathbb E\Bigl[-\log|\Sigma_{z}|+\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma_{z}+\|\mu_{z}\|^{2}-d_{z}\Bigr]
+\frac{1}{2\sigma_{u}^{2}}\mathbb E\Bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{M}
\frac{\bigl(\hat{\mathcal G}(h(\mathcal O),z)(y_{i})-u(y_{i})\bigr)^{2}}{M}\Bigr],
$$

the first bracket being the analytic Gaussian-to-standard-Gaussian KL and the second the reconstruction term, which is proportional to the mean-squared $L^2$ norm for uniform grids and large $M$. $\mathcal L$ upper-bounds $\mathbb E[-\log p_D(\bar u\mid\mathcal O)]$.

**Hard boundary conditions.** Homogeneous Dirichlet conditions are carried by the architecture: on $[-1,1]$ in one dimension, $u(x)=(1-x)(1+x)u_\theta(x)$; on $[0,1]^2$ in two dimensions, $u(x,y)=x(1-x)y(1-y)u_\theta(x,y)$, with $u_\theta$ the raw network output.

**Training and inference.** Each mini-batch picks a sensor count $m\in\mathcal M$ at random, draws $m$ random positions per sample to form $\mathcal O_i$, computes $h(\mathcal O_i)$, samples $z_i\sim q_E$, and forms the stochastic loss

$$
\hat{\mathcal L}=\frac{1}{2B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}
\Bigl(-\log|\Sigma_{z}|+\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma_{z}+\|\mu_{z}\|^{2}-d_{z}\Bigr)
+\frac{1}{2BM\sigma_{u}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{j=1}^{M}
\Bigl(\hat{\mathcal G}(h(\mathcal O_{i}),z_{i})(y_{j})-u(y_{j})\Bigr)^{2},
$$

updated by Adam. **At inference $z$ is drawn from the prior, not the encoder**, since $\bar u$ is unknown at test time; repeatedly drawing $z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ accumulates the conditional distribution of $u$.

### Theorems

**The paper contains no numbered theorem or proposition.** The theoretical content is the ELBO derivation of Section 3.3, Remark 3.1 on scaling $\sigma_u^2$ by $M$, and the functional-VAE limit argument of Appendix A.3.

The theoretical basis for permutation invariance comes from the set-learning background literature rather than from this paper. The relevant statement is Deep Sets Theorem 2: **a set function on a countable universe is permutation-invariant if and only if it decomposes as $\rho\bigl(\sum_{x\in X}\phi(x)\bigr)$** for suitable $\phi$ and $\rho$. The attention pooling here is a softmax-weighted instance of that form.

> [!warning] Where the background verification stops
> Deep Sets Theorem 2 and its scope restriction are verified: the paper itself states that when $\mathfrak X$ is uncountable (for instance $\mathfrak X=\mathbb R$) the decomposition could only be proved for sets of **fixed size**, so the clean "if and only if" is a countable-universe theorem. The MAB/SAB/ISAB/PMA equations of the Set Transformer paper are **not verified** — only its supplementary material was reachable in this pass. The permutation-invariance argument on this page therefore rests on the Deep Sets theorem and on this paper's own Eq. 3.2, not on those unverified formulas.

### Numerical experiments

Global settings: Tanh activation, truncation rank $p=100$, latent dimension $d_z=10$ (chosen because the $W_2$ distance stabilises there, their Fig. 2(a)), and 10,000 training samples for the one-dimensional cases. **All errors are relative $L^2$ errors scaled by $10^{-2}$**, so a reported "5.35" means $5.35\times10^{-2}$.

Evaluation uses the Wasserstein-2 distance

$$
W_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\Bigl(\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{M\times M}d^{2}(x_{1},x_{2})\,\mathrm d\gamma\Bigr)^{1/2}
$$

together with the relative errors of mean and standard deviation,

$$
err_{E[u]}=\frac{\|E[u]-E_{\theta}[u]\|_{2}}{\|E[u]\|_{2}},
\qquad
err_{\sigma[u]}=\frac{\|\sigma[u]-\sigma_{\theta}[u]\|_{2}}{\|\sigma[u]\|_{2}} .
$$

**The reference distributions are not Monte Carlo over the trained model; they are exact Gaussian-process conditionals** (Appendix A.2). Since $\log\kappa\sim\mathcal{GP}(\mu,\mathcal K)$, the posterior given sensor values remains a Gaussian process,

$$
\mu_{\text{post}}(x)=\mu(x)+\mathcal K(x,x_{\text{obs}})
\mathcal K(x_{\text{obs}},x_{\text{obs}})^{-1}(y_{\text{obs}}-\mu(x_{\text{obs}})),
$$

$$
\mathcal K_{\text{post}}(x,x')=\mathcal K(x,x')-\mathcal K(x,x_{\text{obs}})
\mathcal K(x_{\text{obs}},x_{\text{obs}})^{-1}\mathcal K(x_{\text{obs}},x') ,
$$

with the inverse replaced by $[\mathcal K(x_{\text{obs}},x_{\text{obs}})+\sigma^2I]^{-1}$ under additive noise. **1,000 posterior samples are drawn per observation set and pushed through a finite-difference solver** to build the reference conditional distribution. **This is what makes the UQ claims falsifiable rather than self-referential**, and it is worth borrowing as methodology.

**Example 1: 1-D diffusion.** $-\tfrac1{10}\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\bigl(k(x)\tfrac{\mathrm du}{\mathrm dx}\bigr)=f(x)$ on $[-1,1]$ with $u(\pm1)=0$ and $f(x)=2\sin(2\pi x)$; learn $\mathcal G:k\mapsto u$. The input is $\log k\sim\mathcal{GP}\bigl(\sin(2\pi x),\sigma^2\exp(-(x-x')^2/l^2)\bigr)$ with $\sigma=0.5$, $l=0.1$; a 401-point sensor grid, a second-order finite-difference solver, $N=10^4$ pairs, and 101 output points in training. Variable sensor count $\mathcal M=\{1,\dots,10\}$, 100,000 iterations, testing on a 401-point grid, 5 trials.

| $m$ | UQ-SONet $err_{E[u]}$ | VIDON $err_{E[u]}$ |
| --- | --------------------- | ------------------ |
| 1   | 4.47 ± 0.83           | 5.35 ± 0.15        |
| 7   | 5.88 ± 0.74           | 8.73 ± 1.03        |

UQ-SONet beats VIDON at **every** sensor count from 1 to 10, and $err_{\sigma[u]}$ ranges over 7.68–8.95. The predictive bands narrow as $m$ grows. Noise robustness is tested separately (their Table 3): with multiplicative noise $k(x)\exp(\epsilon(x))$, $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$, $\sigma\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,1.0\}$, the errors stay essentially flat — at $m=7$, $err_{E[u]}$ moves from 7.49 to 6.50 as $\sigma$ goes from 0.1 to 1.0. **Degradation is remarkably insensitive to the noise level.**

**Example 2: 2-D Poisson.** $-\tfrac1{10}\Delta u=f$ on $[0,1]^2$ with homogeneous Dirichlet conditions; learn $\mathcal G:f\mapsto u$. Here $f\sim\mathcal{GP}$ with mean $4(\sin2\pi x+\sin2\pi y)$ and $l_1=l_2=0.1$ on a $101\times101$ grid; $N=80{,}000$ pairs; $\mathcal M=\{1,2,3,4\}$; sensors placed by **regular-space clustering** (`pyemma.coordinates.cluster_regspace`) on permuted $81\times81$ grids inside $[0.1,0.9]^2$, with minimum spacing $d_{\min}=0.8$ for $m=2$ and $d_{\min}=0.5$ for $m=3,4$; training output grid $51\times51$, test grid $101\times101$; 20,000 iterations, 3 trials.

| $m$ | UQ-SONet (noiseless) | VIDON (noiseless) | UQ-SONet (noise $\mathcal N(0,1.0^2)$) |
| --- | -------------------- | ----------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| 1   | 4.00                 | 3.26              | 3.40                                   |
| 2   | 4.83                 | 4.67              | 3.40                                   |
| 3   | 5.92                 | 5.99              | 4.16                                   |
| 4   | 5.48                 | 7.01              | 5.54                                   |

**This is the most honest table in the paper: VIDON is actually better at $m=1,2$**, and UQ-SONet wins only at $m=3,4$. The claim that adding uncertainty comes with better accuracy for free is therefore not unconditional.

**Example 3: 1-D elliptic SDE.** $-\tfrac1{10}\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\bigl(k(x;\omega)\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}u(x;\omega)\bigr)=f(x;\omega)$, with $\log k$ given by $l=0.05$, $\sigma=0.3$, $\mu=\sin(\pi x+1)$ and $f$ by $l=0.1$, $\sigma=0.1$, $\mu=\sin(2\pi x)+0.1$; learn $\mathcal G:k\mapsto u$, so **$f$ is a second, unobserved source of randomness**. Across $m=1,\dots,10$ with 5 trials, $err_{E[u]}$ falls in 1.76–2.45 and $err_{\sigma[u]}$ in 3.61–5.66 — the best accuracy in the paper. The qualitatively important observation is that **the predictive spread does not shrink to zero as $m\to10$**, because the intrinsic randomness of $f$ persists while only the epistemic part is resolved. This is the cleanest evidence in the paper that the model separates the two uncertainty sources.

**Example 4: 2-D elliptic SDE.** $-\nabla\cdot(k(x,y;\omega)\nabla u)=f(x,y;\omega)$ on $[0,1]^2$, with $\log k$ zero-mean and $l_1=l_2=0.1$ and $f$ of mean $4(\sin2\pi x+\sin2\pi y)$; learn $\mathcal G:f\mapsto u$; $\mathcal M=\{1,2,3,4\}$, 50,000 iterations, training grid $51\times51$, test grid $101\times101$. Evaluated along the slices $x=0.5,0.7$ and $y=0.5,0.7$ over 3 trials, $err_{E[u]}$ falls in 3.23–7.57 and $err_{\sigma[u]}$ in 6.52–8.50.

**Example 5: 2-D incompressible Navier–Stokes.** Vorticity–velocity form with periodic boundaries, $\partial_tw+u\cdot\nabla w=\nu\Delta w+f$, $\nabla\cdot u=0$, $\nu=0.001$, $f(x,y)=0.1\sin(2\pi(x+y))+0.1\cos(2\pi(x+y))$; learn $w_0\mapsto w|_{t=10}$. **Deep-ensemble baseline (Appendix A.4).** Built by retraining VIDON with different seeds and data shuffling. ### Relation to the others

A direct methodological sibling of paper 89 (the same Ling Guo / Hao Wu / Tao Zhou trio): both are VAE-style latent-variable models trained by an ELBO with a Gaussian decoder and a diagonal-Gaussian variational posterior. The difference is what the latent buys — in paper 89 the encoder is replaced by a diffusion process to **tighten** a reverse-KL bound for sampling, whereas here the encoder is a conventional amortised Gaussian and the latent carries **predictive** uncertainty.

A direct sibling of paper 98 as well: both attach uncertainty to a neural-operator solution field, and both use an encoder producing a stochastic latent decoded to a conditional Gaussian over the solution. Paper 98 replaces the standard Gaussian latent prior with a Gaussian-process prior blended by a learned confidence function; paper 95 keeps $\mathcal N(0,I)$ and makes the **conditioning** permutation-invariant. Read together they cover the two natural axes: richer prior (98) versus richer conditioning (95).

The introduction of paper 95 explicitly cites the information-bottleneck UQ framework for function regression and operator learning, which is paper 75 — prior work by an overlapping group rather than part of this paper. It shares the DeepONet substrate with papers 94 and 107, and paper 107 is the closest in intent: both learn a **distribution-valued** object, paper 107 via a conditional normalizing flow over transition densities and paper 95 via a conditional VAE over solution fields. It contrasts with the [[en/computational-mathematics/paper-notes/scientific-machine-learning/variational-and-basis-networks|least-squares collocation solvers]] (papers 90 and 102) and with papers 101 and 103: those need the PDE at solve time and produce one deterministic field, whereas paper 95 is purely data-driven, needs no PDE at inference, and produces a distribution.

## 98: replacing the noise term by a Gaussian-process prior

### The idea

The most valuable thing in this paper is the argument it gives in Section 3.1, not the module it swaps in.

Suppose you want to attach uncertainty to a PDE solution with a latent-variable model. The cheapest thing to do is give each point $\bm x$ its own independent noise. The trouble is that solutions are not generated that way. Consider $\lambda u_{xx}=f$: the solution is a **double integral** of $f$, and hence (a relation the paper explicitly labels heuristic)

$$
\mathrm{Var}\bigl(u(x)\bigr)\ \sim\ \iint_{\Omega_x}\mathrm{Var}\bigl(f(\xi)\bigr)\,\mathrm d\xi .
$$

Uncertainty in $u$ is a **global aggregation** of uncertainty in $f$, not a pointwise reflection of it. A low-dimensional i.i.d. latent variable cannot express that; a latent **process** with spatial correlation can. **This is the actual argument for replacing $\mathcal N(0,I)$ by a Gaussian process**, rather than a general appeal to correlation being more realistic.

The paper's criticisms of the existing routes are equally specific: plain Gaussian-process regression struggles with nonlinear equations and scales poorly; Bayesian PINNs with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo are accurate but the posterior computation is expensive; deep ensembles are cheap but produce spurious oscillations and unreliable confidence when data are scarce or noisy. Its summary is that most existing methods "either lack a structured mechanism for representing latent functional uncertainty or remain computationally burdensome".

### Setting

For $\mathcal N_{\bm x}(u;\lambda)=f$ on $\Omega$ and $\mathcal B_{\bm x}(u;\lambda)=b$ on $\partial\Omega$, the PINN baseline takes the residual $r(\bm x;\theta)=\mathcal N_{\bm x}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\bm x;\theta);\lambda_{\mathrm{NN}}(\bm x;\theta))-f(\bm x)$ and the loss $\mathcal L(\theta)=w_r\mathcal L_r+w_b\mathcal L_b+w_d\mathcal L_d$, each term a half sum of squares. Observations are noisy: $\bar u^{(i)}=u(\bm x_u^{(i)})+\epsilon_u^{(i)}$, $\bar f^{(i)}=f(\bm x_f^{(i)})+\epsilon_f^{(i)}$, $\bar b^{(i)}=b(\bm x_b^{(i)})+\epsilon_b^{(i)}$.

### Derivation

**Step one: the confidence-aware encoder (their Eq. 6).**

$$
\bm z(\bm x;\theta_E)=\mathrm{diag}\bigl(m(\bm x;\theta_m)\bigr)\,\bar{\bm z}(\bm x;\theta_{\bar{\bm z}})
+\mathrm{diag}\bigl(1-m(\bm x;\theta_m)\bigr)\,\bm z_0,
$$

where $\bar{\bm z}$ is a deterministic deep network, each component of $m$ lies in $[0,1]$, and $\bm z_0:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^{d_z}$ is a **vector-valued Gaussian process**

$$
\bm z_0\sim\mathcal{GP}\bigl(0,K(\bm x,\bm x')\bigr),
\qquad
K(\bm x,\bm x')=\sigma_K^2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\|\bm x-\bm x'\|^2}{2\ell^2}\Bigr)\bm I_{d_z},
$$

with independent output dimensions sharing one scalar squared-exponential kernel ($\sigma_K=1$ throughout). $m(\bm x)=1$ means $\bm x$ is near training data and $\bm z$ is deterministic; $m(\bm x)=0$ means $\bm x$ is far from data and $\bm z$ inherits the full GP variance. Since $\mathrm{Var}(\bm z(\bm x))=(1-m(\bm x))^2$, the paper notes that $m$ can also be read as **an alternative parameterisation of a Gaussian process with a non-stationary kernel**, the induced encoder law being

$$
q_E(\bm z\mid\bm x)\sim\mathcal N\Bigl(m(\bm x)\bar{\bm z}(\bm x),\ \operatorname{diag}\bigl(1-m(\bm x)\bigr)^2\Bigr).
$$

This is exactly where it differs from papers 95 and 89: the latent prior is a process over the input domain rather than $\mathcal N(0,I)$, so latent randomness is spatially correlated, and the blend weight is learned per input, so the model can be confident where it has seen data and uncertain elsewhere.

**Step two: the neural-operator decoder (their Eqs. 7–8, 10).** The latent field is propagated by an FNO-style integral-operator stack with $\bm z_1(\bm x,\bm\omega_E)=\bm z(\bm x,\bm\omega_E)$, $\bm\omega_E\sim\mathcal N(0,\bm I_M)$:

$$
\bm z_i=\sigma\Bigl(W_i\bm z_{i-1}+b_i+\int_{\Omega}k_i(\bm x,\bm x')\bm z_{i-1}(\bm x',\bm\omega_E)\,\mathrm d\bm x'\Bigr),
\quad i=2,\dots,L-1,
\qquad
\bm z_L=W_L\bm z_{L-1}+b_L,
$$

with a "positional Transformer" kernel

$$
k_i(\bm x,\bm x')=\frac{\exp(-\alpha_i\|\bm x-\bm x'\|^2)}{\int_{\Omega}\exp(-\alpha_i\|\bm x-\bm y\|^2)\mathrm d\bm y}\,V_i,
$$

$\alpha_i>0$ a length scale and $V_i$ a learnable projection; the integral is evaluated by quadrature, FFT or Monte Carlo. The output $\mu_u(\bm x,\bm\omega_E):=\bm z_L(\bm x,\bm\omega_E)$ is the predicted mean of $u$. **This integral layer is the architectural counterpart of the "global aggregation" argument**: the value of $\mu_u$ at $\bm x$ depends on the latent field over the whole domain, not just on $\bm z(\bm x)$.

**Step three: the probabilistic decoder and the separation of the two uncertainties (their Eqs. 9–10).**

$$
q_D^u(u\mid\bm x,\bm\omega_E)\sim\mathcal N\bigl(\mu_u(\bm x,\bm\omega_E),\ \sigma_u^2(\bm x;\theta_\sigma^u)\bigr),
\qquad
u(\bm x,\bm\omega_u)=\bm z_L(\bm x,\bm\omega_E)+\sigma_u(\bm x;\theta_\sigma^u)\cdot\omega_D^u,
$$

with $\omega_D^u\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. So $\sigma_u$ models **aleatoric** uncertainty and $\bm\omega_E$ — through $m$ and the Gaussian process — models **epistemic** uncertainty, and the two are structurally separated.

**Step four: physics as a hard structural identity (their Eqs. 11–12).** The paper assumes $\sigma_u(\bm x;\theta_\sigma^u)$ is _not_ differentiated in $\bm x$, so

$$
\mathcal N_{\bm x}[u(\bm x,\bm\omega_u)]=\mathcal N_{\bm x}[\mu_u(\bm x,\bm\omega_E)],
\qquad
\mu_f=\mathcal N_{\bm x}[\mu_u],
\qquad
\mu_b=\mathcal B_{\bm x}[\mu_u],
$$

$$
f(\bm x,\bm\omega_f)=\mathcal N_{\bm x}[\mu_u]+\sigma_f(\bm x;\theta_\sigma^f)\omega_D^f,
\qquad
b(\bm x,\bm\omega_b)=\mathcal B_{\bm x}[\mu_u]+\sigma_b(\bm x;\theta_\sigma^b)\omega_D^b .
$$

The physical constraint is therefore a **hard structural identity** rather than a second residual network to be fitted — unlike paper 75, which puts the physics in a loss term. The chain rule has to pass through the integral layers, and differentiating $\bm z_1$ requires differentiating the GP sample $\bm z_0$; Appendix A gives two routes for that, **an exact Karhunen–Loève expansion** and **a finite-dimensional joint-Gaussian sampling identity**.

**Step five: the data likelihood.** $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data},u}+\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data},f}+\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data},b}$, each of the form

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data},u}=\frac{1}{N_uN_{\bm\omega}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_u}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\bm\omega}}
\log q_D^u\bigl(\bar u^{(i)}\bigm|\bm x_u^{(i)},\bm\omega_E^{(j)};\theta_u\bigr),
\qquad \bm\omega_E^{(j)}\sim\mathcal N(0,\bm I_M).
$$

**Step six: an information-bottleneck regulariser, needed to stop latent collapse (their Eqs. 13–15).** The paper observes that with the data loss alone the model drives $m(\bm x_i)\to1$ at observation points, collapsing $\bm z$ to a deterministic map over the whole domain. Following paper 75, it adds

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{reg}}(Z,X)=\mathbb E_{\bm x\sim\mathcal U(\Omega)}\,
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[q_E(\bm z\mid\bm x)\,\|\,e_1(\bm z)\bigr],
\qquad e_1(\bm z)\sim\mathcal N(0,\bm I_{d_z}),
$$

estimated empirically as $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{reg}}=\frac1N\sum_i[\log q_E(\bm z_i\mid\bm x_i)-\log e_1(\bm z_i)]$ with $\bm x_i\sim\mathcal U(\Omega)$, giving the objective

$$
\mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}-\beta\cdot\mathcal L_{\mathrm{reg}} .
$$

> [!warning] A sign convention at odds with the prose
> The paper **maximises** $\mathcal L$ (Algorithm 1 uses $W=W+lr\cdot\partial\mathcal L/\partial W$), and $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}$ is written with $+\log q_D$, so it is a log-likelihood rather than a negative log-likelihood, despite the surrounding prose calling it a "negative log-likelihood loss". Training is carried out "in a non-Bayesian manner, without requiring posterior inference".

**Step seven: the correlated regulariser (their Remark 3.3, Eqs. 16–17) — the version actually used in the experiments.** Because $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{reg}}$ ignores interactions across inputs, the paper defines a batch version against a Gaussian-process reference $e_2(\bm z_{1:B}\mid\bm x_{1:B})\sim\mathcal N(0,\bm K)$ with $\bm K=(K(\bm x_i,\bm x_j))_{i,j\le B}$, giving the closed form

$$
\widetilde{\mathcal L}_{\mathrm{reg}}=\mathbb E_{\bm x_{1:B}\sim\mathcal U(\Omega^B)}\frac{1}{2B}
\Bigl[-2\log\det(\bm D_m)-B+\mathrm{tr}(\bm K^{-1}\bm D_m\bm K\bm D_m)
+(\bm{m\bar z})^{\top}\bm K^{-1}(\bm{m\bar z})\Bigr],
$$

with $\bm D_m=\operatorname{diag}(1-m(\bm x_1),\dots,1-m(\bm x_B))$ and $\bm{m\bar z}=(m(\bm x_1)\bar z(\bm x_1),\dots)$. Minimising it optimises $m$ **and the GP hyperparameters simultaneously**. The paper states explicitly that "the statistical properties of $\widetilde{\mathcal L}_{\mathrm{reg}}$ in the limit $B\to\infty$ remain to be theoretically analyzed" — an acknowledged gap.

**Step eight: the inverse-problem extension (their Eqs. 18–20).** An extra decoder $q_D^\lambda(\lambda\mid\bm x,\bm\omega_E;\theta_\lambda)\sim\mathcal N(\mu_\lambda,\sigma_\lambda^2)$ **shares the same encoder randomness $\bm\omega_E$** as $u$; when $\lambda$ is a vector rather than a field, $\mu_\lambda(\bm\omega_E)=\int_\Omega\mu_\lambda(\bm x,\bm\omega_E)\mathrm d\bm x$. Then $f=\mathcal N_{\bm x}[\mu_u;\mu_\lambda]+\sigma_f\omega_D^f$ and $b=\mathcal B_{\bm x}[\mu_u;\mu_\lambda]+\sigma_b\omega_D^b$, and a term $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data},\lambda}$ joins the objective.

**An alternative decoder (their Remark 3.1).** A DeepONet-style decoder $\mu_u(\bm x,\bm\omega_E)=\mathrm{Branch}(\bm z(\bm x_{\mathrm{grid},1},\bm\omega_E),\dots,\bm z(\bm x_{\mathrm{grid},p},\bm\omega_E))\cdot\mathrm{Trunk}(\bm z(\bm x,\bm\omega_E))$ is also possible, but the paper notes the branch input dimension $p\times d_z$ can be very large. The FNO-style integral costs $\mathcal O(N^2)$ naively and $\mathcal O(N\log N)$ with FFT. Results for that variant are in Appendix B.

### Theorems

**No theorems, no propositions, no error analysis.** The conclusion says so explicitly: "a rigorous analysis of stability and accuracy is still lacking, the mathematical well-posedness of the revised regularization term has not yet been fully established". The stated future work is theory, time-dependent PDEs, and folding the framework into physics-informed **operator** learning.

### Numerical experiments

Almost every case is run at two noise levels, $\mathcal N(0,0.01^2)$ and $\mathcal N(0,0.1^2)$, and comparisons are always against B-PINN-HMC and deep ensembles with **identical network architectures**.

**Example 1: 1-D Poisson (forward).** $\lambda\partial_x^2u=f$ on $[-0.7,0.7]$, $\lambda=0.01$, exact solution $u(x)=\sin^3(6x)$; 32 equispaced $f$-sensors plus 2 boundary $u$-sensors; $\sigma_f$ and $\sigma_u$ are learnable scalars **initialised at twice the empirical noise level**. All three methods do well at low noise; at $\sigma=0.1$ deep ensembles fail to give reliable UQ for $f$, and LVM-GP gives a better mean and **tighter** uncertainty than B-PINN-HMC. With the correlated regulariser and a learnable kernel length scale $L_c$: **$L_c$ starts near 1.0 and settles around 0.58 after roughly 8000 iterations** — concrete evidence that the model adapts its own prior correlation scale.

**Example 2: 1-D flow through porous media with a boundary layer (forward).** $-\frac{\nu_e}{\phi}\partial_x^2u+\frac{\nu u}{K}=g$ on $[0,1]$ with $u(0)=u(1)=0$ and analytic solution

$$
u=\frac{gK}{\nu}\Bigl[1-\frac{\cosh\bigl(r(x-H/2)\bigr)}{\cosh(rH/2)}\Bigr],
\qquad r=\sqrt{\nu\phi/(\nu_eK)},
$$

with $\nu_e=\nu=10^{-3}$, $\phi=0.4$, $K=10^{-3}$, $g=1$; 16 $g$-sensors plus 2 boundary sensors; three-layer fully connected encoder and decoder with 20 neurons per hidden layer. Outcome: LVM-GP is comparable to HMC, while deep ensembles are inaccurate and their UQ unreliable at high noise. Training uses a two-stage schedule: **first optimise the predictive mean only for 2,000 steps, then optimise the standard deviations while fine-tuning the mean for 8,000 steps.**

**Example 3: 1-D nonlinear Poisson (inverse).** $k\partial_x^2u+\lambda\tanh(u)=f$ on $[-0.7,0.7]$, $k=0.01$, exact $u=\sin^3(6x)$, unknown reaction rate $\lambda=0.7$; 32 $f$-sensors, 2 boundary $u$-sensors, 6 interior $u$-sensors; the $\Lambda$ network is a single-neuron hidden layer with $\tanh$.

| Noise | LVM-GP mean (std)             | B-PINN-HMC                    | Deep ensemble                 |
| ----- | ----------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| 0.01  | 0.6976 ($9.816\times10^{-3}$) | 0.6967 ($4.225\times10^{-3}$) | 0.6966 ($2.493\times10^{-4}$) |

### Relation to the others

A sibling of paper 95 (both from the Ling Guo / Hao Wu / Tao Zhou line): both are encoder–latent–decoder models with a conditional-Gaussian decoder over the solution field and a neural-operator mean. The split is clean — paper 98 enriches the **prior** (a Gaussian process instead of $\mathcal N(0,I)$, with a learned confidence blend) and trains non-Bayesianly with an IB-style KL regulariser; paper 95 keeps the standard Gaussian prior and enriches the **conditioning** (a permutation-invariant set transformer over sensors), trained by a proper ELBO.

A sibling of paper 89 in machinery (latent variable, KL divergence, reparameterised sampling) but opposite in purpose: paper 89 samples from a known energy, paper 98 regresses a PDE solution from noisy data. The information-bottleneck objective $I(Z;Y)-\beta I(Z;X)$ is written out explicitly in Section 2.2.2 of this paper and cited to paper 75, and $\mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}-\beta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is a variational instance of it.

It contrasts with the [[en/computational-mathematics/paper-notes/scientific-machine-learning/variational-and-basis-networks|deterministic PDE solvers]] (papers 90 and 102) and with papers 101 and 103: those assume exact PDE data and drive the residual to machine-level accuracy by least squares, whereas paper 98 assumes **noisy, sparse** data where driving the residual to zero would be overfitting, and treats the physics as a soft constraint inside a likelihood.

## 107: changing the learned object from initial-condition dependence to the transition density

### The idea

In ensemble forecasting and data assimilation the stochastic dynamics is fixed while the **initial distribution changes** with each scenario or posterior update. Treating "initial distribution $\mapsto$ time-dependent solution" as the operator to be learned means preparing fresh training data for every new family of initial conditions.

This paper changes the learned object. Instead of the initial-condition dependence it learns the **transition density** $p(\bm x,t\mid\bm x_0)$ — the density of landing at $\bm x$ after time $t$ having started from a definite point $\bm x_0$. That depends only on the dynamics, not on the initial distribution. With it in hand, the solution for any initial distribution follows from a single integral against the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation, with no retraining at all.

The price appears immediately: the transition density solves an equation with a **Dirac initial condition**. A normalizing flow's output density is always smooth, and no invertible map can send a smooth base distribution to $\delta(\bm x-\bm x_0)$. The paper's answer is to hand the singularity to the **base distribution**: linearise the drift at $\bm x_0$, freeze the diffusion, and the resulting linear SDE has a closed-form Gaussian solution which collapses to $\delta_{\bm x_0}$ automatically as $t\to0$. The flow then only has to learn a **near-identity** correction on top of a base that is already approximately right.

The same thought, taken one step further, gives the second trick: make every nonlinear term in the coupling layer carry an explicit factor of $t$, so the layer is **exactly** the identity at $t=0$ and the initial condition holds without any penalty term. The third trick is time weighting: however good the approximation, the PDE residual itself blows up as $t\to0$, so the loss must be weighted against that blow-up rate.

### Setting

Let the non-degenerate SDE be

$$
\mathrm d\bm X_t=\bm f(\bm X_t)\mathrm dt+\bm g(\bm X_t)\mathrm d\bm W_t,
\qquad \bm f:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d,\quad \bm g:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^{d\times m},\ m\ge d,
$$

whose density obeys the Fokker–Planck equation

$$
\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=\mathcal L^{*}_{\bm f,\bm g}p
:=-\nabla\cdot(p\bm f)+\frac12\nabla\cdot\nabla\cdot(\bm g\bm g^{\mathrm T}p),
$$

with $p(\bm x)\to0$ as $|\bm x|_2\to\infty$, $\int_{\mathbb R^d}p(\bm x,t)\mathrm d\bm x\equiv1$ and $p\ge0$. The paper argues that applying DeepONet, Fourier neural operators or Green's-function operator learning directly to Fokker–Planck equations "requires additional treatments of boundary conditions and PDF constraints" (non-negativity and unit mass).

### Derivation

**Step one: learn the transition density instead.** Rather than solving for each $p_0\in\mathcal G$ in a whole class, solve once for

$$
\frac{\partial p(\bm x,t\mid\bm x_{0})}{\partial t}
=\mathcal L^{*}_{\bm f,\bm g}\,p(\bm x,t\mid\bm x_{0}),
\qquad
p(\bm x,0\mid\bm x_{0})=\delta(\bm x-\bm x_{0}),
$$

then recover any initial condition through the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation

$$
p(\bm x,t)=\int_{\Omega_{0}}p(\bm x,t\mid\bm x_{0})\,p_{0}(\bm x_{0})\,\mathrm d\bm x_{0} .
$$

**This is the core move: the learned object is independent of $p_0$, so new initial data need no retraining.**

**Step two: a conditional normalizing flow.** From the change of variables $p_{\bm X}(\bm x)=p_{\bm Z}(T(\bm x))|\det\nabla_{\bm x}T(\bm x)|$, let both $T$ and the base law depend on $(\bm x_0,t)$:

$$
\bm X_t|_{\bm X_0=\bm x_0}=T^{-1}\bigl(\bm Z_t(\bm x_0);\theta(\bm x_0,t)\bigr),
\qquad
p_{\bm X_{t}\mid\bm X_{0}=\bm x_{0}}(\bm x)
=p_{\bm Z_{t}(\bm x_{0})}\bigl(T(\bm x;\theta(\bm x_{0},t))\bigr)\,
\bigl|\det\nabla_{\bm x}T(\bm x;\theta(\bm x_{0},t))\bigr| .
$$

Because it is a flow, non-negativity and unit mass hold **by construction** — the paper's answer to the "PDF constraints" objection, and the same orientation as the [[en/computational-mathematics/paper-notes/scientific-machine-learning/normalizing-flows-for-densities|density-flow page]].

**Step three: the linearised base process.** Take a first-order Taylor expansion of the drift and a zeroth-order expansion of the diffusion, both at $\bm x_0$:

$$
\mathrm d\widehat{\bm X}_{t}
=\bigl(\bm f(\bm x_{0})+\nabla\bm f(\bm x_{0})(\widehat{\bm X}_{t}-\bm x_{0})\bigr)\mathrm dt
+\bm g(\bm x_{0})\mathrm d\bm W_{t},
\qquad \widehat{\bm X}_{0}=\bm x_{0} .
$$

This is a linear Ornstein–Uhlenbeck-type equation whose solution can be written explicitly,

$$
\widehat{\bm X}_{t}=\bm x_{0}
+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\nabla\bm f(\bm x_{0})(t-s)}\bm f(\bm x_{0})\,\mathrm ds
+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\nabla\bm f(\bm x_{0})(t-s)}\bm g(\bm x_{0})\,\mathrm d\bm W_{s},
$$

so it is a Gaussian process with closed-form mean and covariance:

$$
\mathbb E\bigl[\widehat{\bm X}_{t}\bigm|\widehat{\bm X}_0=\bm x_0\bigr]
=\bm x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\nabla\bm f(\bm x_{0})(t-s)}\bm f(\bm x_{0})\,\mathrm ds,
$$

$$
\bm\Sigma\bigl[\widehat{\bm X}_{t}\bigm|\widehat{\bm X}_0=\bm x_0\bigr]
=\int_{0}^{t}e^{\nabla\bm f(\bm x_{0})(t-s)}\bm g(\bm x_{0})\bm g(\bm x_{0})^{\top}
e^{\nabla\bm f(\bm x_{0})^{\top}(t-s)}\,\mathrm ds .
$$

Both integrals are evaluated by **Gauss–Legendre quadrature**: the standard nodes and weights $\{(\hat w_i,\hat s_i)\}$ on $[-1,1]$ are mapped by $w_i=\tfrac t2\hat w_i$, $s_i=\tfrac t2(\hat s_i+1)$. When $\nabla\bm f(\bm x_0)$ is invertible the mean also has the closed form $\bm x_0+(\nabla\bm f(\bm x_0))^{-1}(e^{\nabla\bm f(\bm x_0)t}-\bm I)\bm f(\bm x_0)$. **As $t\to0$ the covariance vanishes and the mean tends to $\bm x_0$, so this Gaussian collapses automatically to $\delta_{\bm x_0}$** — the flow never has to bridge from a smooth distribution to a Dirac mass, because the base absorbs the singularity.

**Step four: the C-KRnet architecture.** The flow is a composition $T=T_{[K]}\circ\cdots\circ T_{[1]}$ with $|\det\nabla_{\bm x}T|=\prod_i|\det\nabla_{\bm x_{[i-1]}}T_{[i]}|$, using the **descending-active-dimension** KRnet structure (Knothe–Rosenblatt rearrangement plus affine coupling layers):

$$
T_{[1]}=\tilde T_{K},
\qquad
T_{[k]}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde T_{K+1-k}\\ \mathrm{Id}_{K+2-k:K}\end{pmatrix},
\quad k=2,\dots,K,
$$

for a partition $\bm x^{\top}=((\bm x^{(1)})^{\top},\dots,(\bm x^{(K)})^{\top})$ with $\sum_id_i=d$. The outer loop has $K$ stages, the inner map $\tilde T_k$ has $l_k$ coupling layers, and the $k$-th partition is frozen after $\tilde T_k$, so later stages can use smaller $l_k$.

**Step five: a conditional coupling layer that is the identity at $t=0$ (their Eqs. 15–16).**

$$
\hat{\bm y}_{1}=\bm y_{1},
\qquad
\hat{\bm y}_{2}=\bm y_{2}\odot\bigl(\bm 1_{m-m_{1}}+\beta\tanh(t\cdot\bm s)\bigr)
+e^{\bm\zeta}\odot\tanh(t\cdot\bm q),
\qquad
(\bm s,\bm q)=\mathrm{NN}(\bm y_{1},\bm\xi,t),
$$

with $\beta\in(0,1)$ user-defined and $\bm\zeta$ trainable. Because both $\tanh$ arguments carry an explicit factor $t$, **the layer is exactly the identity at $t=0$**, matching the base distribution and enforcing the initial condition without any penalty term. The roles of $\bm y_1$ and $\bm y_2$ alternate across layers so that all coordinates get updated.

**Step six: a random-Fourier-feature conditioning network (their Eq. 17).**

$$
\bm h_{0}=[\bm y_{1},\bm\xi,t]^{\top},
\qquad
\bm h_{1}=\Bigl[\sin\bigl(\tfrac{1}{e^{\gamma}}\bm F\bm h_{0}+\bm b_{0}\bigr),\
\cos\bigl(\tfrac{1}{e^{\gamma}}\bm F\bm h_{0}+\bm b_{0}\bigr),\ \bm h_{0}\Bigr]^{\top},
$$

$$
\bm h_{j}=\mathrm{SiLU}(\bm W_{j-1}\bm h_{j-1}+\bm b_{j-1}),\ j=2,\dots,M,
\qquad
(\bm s,\bm q)=\bm W_{M}\bm h_{M}+\bm b_{M},
$$

with $\bm F\in\mathbb R^{r_h/2\times\dim(h_0)}$ drawn from a standard normal and $\bm b_0\in\mathbb R^{r_h/2}$ drawn uniformly on $[0,2\pi]^{r_h/2}$, both **fixed after initialisation**, while $\gamma$ and $\{\bm W_j,\bm b_j\}$ are trainable. The stated motivation is to emphasise the influence of the initial state $\bm x_0$. Note that the frequency scale $1/e^{\gamma}$ is itself learnable.

**Step seven: residual loss and adaptive sampling.** The loss has only a residual term (their Eq. 18):

$$
\mathcal L(\bm\theta)=\int_{\mathbb R^{d}\times(0,t_{f}]\times\Omega_{0}}
|r(\bm x,\bm x_{0},t;p_{\bm\theta})|^{2}\,\mathrm d\rho(\bm x,\bm x_{0},t),
\qquad
r=\frac{\partial p_{\bm\theta}}{\partial t}-\mathcal L^{*}_{\bm f,\bm g}[p_{\bm\theta}],
$$

with the sampling measure factorised as $\rho(\bm x,\bm x_0,t)=\rho(\bm x\mid\bm x_0,t)\rho(t\mid\bm x_0)\rho(\bm x_0)$, where $\rho(t\mid\bm x_0)\rho(\bm x_0)$ is uniform on $\Omega_0\times[0,t_f]$. **There is no initial-condition loss term** — it holds structurally. The spatial part is updated stagewise (their Eq. 19):

$$
\rho_{0}(\bm x\mid\bm x_{0},t)=\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{3}}\mu(\bm x)
+\frac{\gamma_{3}}{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{3}}\rho_{\bm Z}(\bm x\mid\bm x_{0},t),
$$

$$
\rho_{k+1}(\bm x\mid\bm x_{0},t)=\gamma_{1}\mu(\bm x)+\gamma_{2}\rho_{k}(\bm x\mid\bm x_{0},t)
+\gamma_{3}\rho_{\text{C-KRnet},\bm\theta}(\bm x\mid\bm x_{0},t),
\qquad \gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}=1,
$$

mixing a uniform measure $\mu$ on a user-defined box, the previous stage's measure, and the measure induced by the **current** flow. Stage zero seeds from the **base process** $\rho_{\bm Z}$ rather than from the uniform measure, precisely because the base is accurate at small $t$.

**Step eight: time weighting.** Proposition 4.1 says the residual diverges as $t\to0$ at two different rates, and those two rates correspond to **two different sampling measures**. The empirical loss therefore uses two different exponents (their Eq. 22): weight $w_1(t)=t^{\frac d2+2}$ on the uniformly-sampled part and $w_2(t)=t^{d+2}$ on the two solution-sampled parts,

$$
\widehat{\mathcal L}_{w}^{k}(\bm\theta;S)
:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl|r(\bm x^{i},\bm x^{i}_{0},t^{i};p_{\bm\theta})\bigr|^{2}
\Bigl(\eta^{i}t^{d+2}+(1-\eta^{i})t^{\frac d2+2}\Bigr),
$$

with $N=N_1+N_2+N_3$, $N_1:N_2:N_3=\gamma_1:\gamma_2:\gamma_3$, and indicator $\eta^i=0$ for the uniform samples $S_1$ and $\eta^i=1$ for $S_2,S_3$. The paper describes the weighting as "a trade-off between maintaining causality and alleviating optimization difficulties" — the exponents exactly cancel the blow-up rates of Proposition 4.1, making the weighted residual $\mathcal O(1)$.

**Step nine: importance sampling at evaluation time (their Eqs. 24–27).** Plain Monte Carlo $p(\bm x,t)\approx\frac1M\sum_ip(\bm x,t\mid\bm x_0^i)$ with $\bm x_0^i\sim p_0$ has high variance, because at small $t$ the transition density is nearly a Dirac and almost every $\bm x_0^i$ contributes essentially nothing at a given $\bm x$. Instead,

$$
p(\bm x,t)\approx\hat p_{\bm\theta}(\bm x,t)
:=\frac1M\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{p(\bm x,t\mid\bm x_{0}^{i})p_{0}(\bm x_{0}^{i})}{q(\bm x_{0}^{i}\mid\bm x,t)},
\qquad \bm x_{0}^{i}\sim q(\cdot\mid\bm x,t),
$$

with the "time-reversed linearised" proposal

$$
q_{1}(\bm x_{0}\mid\bm x,t)=\mathcal N\bigl(\bm x_{0};m(\bm x,t),\tilde{\bm\Sigma}(\bm x,t)\bigr),
$$

$$
m(\bm x,t)=\bm x-\int_{0}^{t}e^{\nabla\bm f(\bm x)s}\bm f(\bm x)\,\mathrm ds,
\qquad
\tilde{\bm\Sigma}(\bm x,t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{\nabla\bm f(\bm x)s}\bm g(\bm x)\bm g(\bm x)^{\top}
e^{\nabla\bm f(\bm x)^{\top}s}\,\mathrm ds .
$$

Because $q_1$ degrades as $t$ grows, a **time-annealed mixture** is used instead,

$$
q_{2}(\bm x_{0}\mid\bm x,t)=\alpha(t)q_{1}(\bm x_{0}\mid\bm x,t)+(1-\alpha(t))p_{0}(\bm x_{0}),
\qquad \alpha(t)=\exp(-at),\ a>0,
$$

sampled by drawing $\alpha(t)M$ points from $q_1$ and $(1-\alpha(t))M$ from $p_0$.

**The algorithm.** The outer loop runs $k=0,\dots,N_{\mathrm{adaptive}}$. At $k=0$, set $N_1=\lfloor\frac{\gamma_1}{\gamma_1+\gamma_3}N\rfloor$ and $N_3=N-N_1$, generate $S_1$ from $\mu(\bm x)\rho(t\mid\bm x_0)\rho(\bm x_0)$ with $\eta^i=0$ and $S_3$ from the current flow with $\eta^i=1$, and set $S=S_1\cup S_3$. For $k\ge1$, set $N_1=\lfloor\gamma_1N\rfloor$, $N_2=\lfloor\gamma_2N\rfloor$, $N_3=N-N_1-N_2$, regenerate $S_1$ uniformly, take $S_2$ as $N_2$ random samples **drawn from the existing $S$**, draw $S_3$ from the current flow, and update $S=S_1\cup S_2\cup S_3$. The inner loop runs $N_e$ epochs, decaying the learning rate every $n_s$ steps; each epoch splits $S$ into mini-batches, computes the loss of Eq. 22 and updates with Adam. At evaluation, for any new $p_0$, draw from $q_2$ and assemble $\hat p_{\bm\theta}$ by the importance-sampling formula, with no retraining.

> [!warning] Reused symbols
> In the source $m$ denotes both the number of columns of the diffusion matrix and the number of Gauss–Legendre nodes, and $\eta$ denotes both the learning-rate decay factor and the sample indicator in the loss. Context disambiguates them, but the reader should be warned.

### Theorems

Four propositions, with proofs in Appendices A–D.

**Proposition 3.1 (short-time accuracy of the linearised base).** Assume $\bm f=\bm f_\nu$ is differentiable, $\bm g$ is **constant**, $\bm G=\tfrac12\bm g\bm g^{\top}$ is non-degenerate, and $\|\nabla\bm f_\nu(\bm x)\|\le C_{\bm f}$ for all $\bm x$. Let $\nu_t$ and $\sigma_t$ be the laws of the true and linearised processes started from $\delta_{\bm x_0}$. Then for $t\in(0,t_f)$,

$$
H(\sigma_{t}\mid\nu_{t})\le\frac12\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\mathbb R^{d}}
\bigl|\bm G^{-1/2}(\bm f_{\sigma}(\bm x)-\bm f_{\nu}(\bm x))\bigr|^{2}\mathrm d\sigma_{s}\mathrm ds,
$$

$$
\|\nu_{t}-\sigma_{t}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}\le\frac14\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\mathbb R^{d}}
\bigl|\bm G^{-1/2}(\bm f_{\sigma}-\bm f_{\nu})\bigr|^{2}\mathrm d\sigma_{s}\mathrm ds,
$$

and both $H(\sigma_t\mid\nu_t)$ and $\|\nu_t-\sigma_t\|^2_{\mathrm{TV}}$ are $\mathcal O(t^2)$ as $t\to0$, so the total-variation distance itself is $\mathcal O(t)$. **If in addition $\|\nabla^2\bm f_\nu(\bm x)\|\le H_{\bm f}$, both improve to $\mathcal O(t^3)$.** The proof combines a relative-entropy bound of Bogachev et al. with the Pinsker–Csiszár–Kullback inequality, together with the intermediate estimates

$$
\mathrm{tr}(\bm\Sigma_{s})\le\mathrm{tr}(\bm G)\frac{e^{2C_{\bm f}s}-1}{C_{\bm f}}=\mathcal O(s),
\qquad
\bigl|\mathbb E_{\sigma_s}[\widehat{\bm X}_{s}]-\bm x_{0}\bigr|^{2}
\le|\bm f(\bm x_{0})|^{2}\Bigl(\frac{e^{C_{\bm f}s}-1}{C_{\bm f}}\Bigr)^{2}=\mathcal O(s^{2}).
$$

**Proposition 3.2 (transfer of expectations under total variation).** For probability measures $\nu,\sigma$ on $\mathbb R^d$ and $\bm f\in L^2(\nu)\cap L^2(\sigma)$,

$$
\Bigl|\int\bm f\,\mathrm d\nu-\int\bm f\,\mathrm d\sigma\Bigr|
\le\sqrt{2\|\nu-\sigma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}}
\Bigl(\|\bm f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb R^{d},\nu;\mathbb R^{m})}+\|\bm f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb R^{d},\sigma;\mathbb R^{m})}\Bigr).
$$

**Proposition 3.3 (Knothe–Rosenblatt maps tend to the identity).** If $\{\nu_i\}$ are absolutely continuous with finite second moments, $\mu$ is absolutely continuous, $\|\nu_i-\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\to0$ and $\|\bm x\|_{L^2(\nu_i)}\to\|\bm x\|_{L^2(\mu)}$, then the Knothe–Rosenblatt map $T_i$ with $(T_i)_{\#}\mu=\nu_i$ satisfies $T_i\to\mathrm{Id}$ in $L^2(\mu)$. **This is the theoretical justification for the $t=0$ identity constraint in the coupling layer**: Proposition 3.1 says the base is close in total variation at small $t$, and Proposition 3.3 then says the required transport is nearly the identity, so the flow only has to learn a small perturbation.

**Proposition 4.1 (residual blow-up as $t\to0$ — the justification for the weights).** Under the same hypotheses ($\bm g$ constant, $\bm G$ non-degenerate, $\|\nabla\bm f\|\le C_{\bm f}$), for $\tilde p(\bm x,t\mid\bm x_0)=\mathcal N(\bm x;\bm m(t),\bm\Sigma(t))$ with $\bm\Sigma(t)\sim t\bm G$ and $\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\bm m(t)$ bounded,

$$
\int_{\mathbb R^{d}}|r(\bm x,\bm x_{0},t;\tilde p)|^{2}\mathrm d\bm x=\mathcal O\bigl(t^{-(\frac d2+2)}\bigr),
\qquad
\mathbb E_{\bm x\sim\tilde p}\bigl[|r(\bm x,\bm x_{0},t;\tilde p)|^{2}\bigr]=\mathcal O\bigl(t^{-(d+2)}\bigr).
$$

The point is that the two rates belong to **two different sampling measures**, which is precisely why the empirical loss carries two different exponents. **Even an arbitrarily good approximation has a diverging residual near $t=0$**; only the weighted residual is $\mathcal O(1)$.

### Numerical experiments

Implemented in PyTorch with initial learning rate $0.001$ and 10 Gauss–Legendre quadrature points in Eq. 13. Three metrics are used: the relative $L^2$ error of the transition density, the relative $L^2$ error of $\hat p_{\bm\theta}(\cdot,t)$, and a mixed-bandwidth-kernel maximum mean discrepancy (the kernel being $K(\bm x,\bm y)=\tfrac13\sum$ of Gaussians at bandwidths $\hat\sigma^2/4$, $\hat\sigma^2$ and $4\hat\sigma^2$, with $\hat\sigma$ the median distance). Reference solutions come from Gauss–Kronrod quadrature where the transition density is analytic and otherwise from an **ADI finite-difference scheme** with $\Delta t=0.001$, $\Delta x=0.01$; MMD samples come from Euler–Maruyama with step $0.001$.

**Example 1: 2-D Beneš SDE.** $\mathrm d\bm X_t=\tanh\bm X_t\,\mathrm dt+\bm I_d\mathrm d\bm W_t$ with $\bm x_0\in[-1,1]^d$, whose transition density is known exactly:

$$
p(\bm x,t\mid\bm x_{0})=\frac{1}{(2\pi t)^{d/2}}
\exp\Bigl(-\frac d2t\Bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2t}\|\bm x-\bm x_{0}\|^{2}\Bigr)
\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\cosh x_{i}}{\cosh x_{0,i}} .
$$

Setup: 8 coupling layers, each conditioning network with 32 random Fourier features and two hidden layers of 32 neurons; 10 adaptive iterations of 300 epochs each; Adam with learning rate $0.001$ halved every 2,000 epochs; $2\times10^5$ training points with batch size $10^4$; $10^6$ validation samples.

The ablation over three losses (all with $\gamma_1=0.2$, $\gamma_2=0.6$, $\gamma_3=0.2$):

| Loss          | Time interval | Outcome                                                                                                                             |
| ------------- | ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Vanilla PINN  | $(0,1.5)$     | Its error curve **overlaps the base distribution's** — the residual near $t=0$ dominates, so training there contributes nothing     |
| Vanilla PINN  | $(0.1,1.5)$   | Removing the PDE constraint near $t=0$ improves accuracy for $t>0.1$, but the origin is then covered only by continuity of the flow |
| Time-weighted | $(0,1.5)$     | Accurate across the whole range                                                                                                     |

Snapshots at $t=0.01$, $0.5$ and $1.5$ from $\bm x_0=(0,0)$; at $t=1.5$ the flow captures a **multi-modal** transition density. The error also decays monotonically across adaptive iterations.

**Example 2: Beneš with initial-distribution transfer (2-D).** Initial conditions are taken from a family of product Beta densities on $[-1,1]^d$,

$$
\textbf{Beta}(\bm x;\bm\zeta,\bm\eta)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\bm B(\zeta_{i},\eta_{i})}
\Bigl(\frac{1+x_{i}}{2}\Bigr)^{\zeta_{i}-1}\Bigl(\frac{1-x_{i}}{2}\Bigr)^{\eta_{i}-1},
$$

starting with the uniform case $\bm\zeta=\bm\eta=(1,1)$; validation on $10^4$ grid points over $[-5,5]^2$ with $M\in\{10^4,10^5,10^6\}$. The relative error follows the Monte-Carlo $\mathcal O(M^{-1/2})$ scaling up to $M\le10^5$, **beyond which the transition-density approximation error dominates** — a clean identification of the accuracy ceiling. Comparing proposals: $q_1$ gives far lower variance as $t\to0$ but its variance grows with $t$; $q_2$ (the mixture, $\alpha(t)=e^{-6t}$) balances variance over the whole interval. The authors also point out that **a uniform initial distribution is discontinuous over $\mathbb R^2$ and degrades traditional solvers** while this method is unaffected — that is the robustness argument, and the MMD metric was introduced specifically for such discontinuous $p_0$.

**Example 3: 4-D Beneš.** Three KRnet stages with $l_1=10$, $l_2=8$, $l_3=6$; the same per-layer network; 20 adaptive iterations of 300 epochs; $\gamma_1=0.2$, $\gamma_2=0.4$, $\gamma_3=0.4$; learning rate $0.001$ halved every 4,000 epochs; $2\times10^5$ training samples with batch size $2\times10^4$; $2\times10^6$ validation samples over $[-1,1]^4\times[-5,5]^4$; evaluation with $q_2$, $\alpha(t)=e^{-6t}$, $M=10^4$, on $10^5$ test points in $[-5,5]^4$. **A practical detail worth recording: the residual is evaluated on a solution scaled by a constant factor of 1,000 to avoid numerical underflow**, because the Fokker–Planck solution becomes very small in higher dimensions.

**Example 4: nonlinear drift with constant diffusion (2-D).**

$$
\mathrm d(X_1,X_2)^{\top}=(2X_2,\ 2X_1-0.8X_2-0.2X_1^{3})^{\top}\mathrm dt
+\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{0.4},\sqrt{0.8})\,\mathrm d\bm W_{t},
$$

with the same architecture and training as the 2-D Beneš case; evaluation with $q_2$, $\alpha(t)=e^{-6t}$, $M=10^4$; validation on $10^4$ grid points over $[-6,6]^2$ against ADI, MMD with $5\times10^4$ samples. Outcome: highly accurate at small $t$ (where the base is close to the truth) and reliable as $t$ grows.

**Example 5: nonlinear drift with state-dependent diffusion (2-D).**

$$
\mathrm d(X_1,X_2)^{\top}=(X_2,\ -0.5X_1-0.3X_1^{3}+\sin X_2)^{\top}\mathrm dt
+\mathrm{diag}(0.5+0.3X_1,\ 0.4+0.1\sin X_2)\,\mathrm d\bm W_{t},
$$

with $\bm x_0\in[-1,1]^2$; 4 adaptive iterations of 1,000 epochs; $4\times10^5$ training points with batch size $5\times10^4$; validation on $10^4$ grid points over $[-5,5]^2$ against ADI, MMD with $5\times10^4$ samples. **The theory (Propositions 3.1 and 4.1) assumes constant $\bm g$, so this example is deliberately outside the analysis** and is presented as a robustness check.

Together these five experiments establish that the linearised base plus the time-weighted loss yields accurate transition densities across the whole time interval, whereas the base alone and vanilla PINN are accurate only at small $t$ and degrade as $t$ grows; and that **no error growth in time is observed**, which the authors attribute to the time weighting. They do not establish any guarantee under state-dependent diffusion, and there is no head-to-head comparison against operator-learning baselines such as DeepONet or the Fourier neural operator.

### Relation to the others

Closest to paper 105 (sharing Xiaoliang Wan and Tao Zhou): both are normalizing-flow methods for stochastic dynamics with an amortisation goal — paper 107 amortises over initial **distributions** by learning the transition kernel, and paper 105 amortises over observation histories by learning forward and backward flows conditioned on a shared summary statistic. Both rely on the Markov structure (Chapman–Kolmogorov here, backward recursion there).

It shares the "make the base distribution do the hard part" design with paper 89: EDG's latent diffusion has an analytically known $p_D(z_t\mid z_0)$ so no SDE solve is needed, and here the base process is an analytically solvable linearised SDE so the flow only learns a near-identity correction. Both also use the change-of-variables and log-determinant machinery for densities.

It shares the operator-learning framing with paper 95: both learn a distribution-valued map so that new inputs need no retraining, paper 95 amortising over sensor observations of a coefficient and paper 107 over the initial law. It shares the **residual-weighting and causality** concern with paper 103 and the PINN literature generally: the $t^{d+2}$ weighting is a temporal reweighting of a PDE residual, in the same family as causal-training weights, but derived from an explicit asymptotic rate (Proposition 4.1) rather than heuristically. The **random Fourier feature** ingredient is shared with paper 101, though paper 101 uses it as a multiscale basis bank while paper 107 uses it to sharpen sensitivity to $\bm x_0$. KRnet, adaptive sampling for Fokker–Planck equations and the $\gamma_1/\gamma_2/\gamma_3$ mixture measure all come from the authors' own prior work; see the [[en/computational-mathematics/paper-notes/scientific-machine-learning/normalizing-flows-for-densities|density-flow page]] and the [[en/computational-mathematics/paper-notes/scientific-machine-learning/adaptive-sampling-for-pinns|adaptive-sampling page]].

> The constructions, losses, algorithms and principal results of all four papers have been checked equation by equation against preprint or journal full texts. How far the quantitative results can be verified differs: the table values for papers 95 and 98 are transcribed item by item; paper 75's error figures and timings live in the figures and in Supplementary S7–S8, which were not transcribed in this pass, so no such numbers appear here; and paper 107's body contains no numerical tables at all — every magnitude sits in Figures 1 and 5–11 — so only setups, ablation structure and qualitative conclusions are recorded. ## The four side by side

| Paper | Where the uncertainty comes from             | How it is represented                      | What enforces the constraints                      |
| ----- | -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| 75    | inputs out of distribution                   | confidence gate + Gaussian decoder         | nothing (soft)                                     |
| 95    | sparse observations or a stochastic operator | latent variable + set transformer          | boundary conditions hard-wired in the architecture |
| 98    | epistemic and aleatoric modelled separately  | Gaussian-process prior + neural operator   | a structural identity (hard)                       |
| 107   | not applicable (varying initial condition)   | conditional flow + linearised base process | density constraints held by the flow               |

The theoretical content differs just as sharply, and is worth tabulating too:

| Paper | Theory                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| ----- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 75    | no theorems; the theory is the variational lower bound and the analysis of why the flattened distribution makes the latent approximately independent of out-of-distribution inputs                                                                                                                  |
| 95    | no theorems; the theory is the ELBO derivation and the function-space limit argument for scaling $\sigma_u^2$ by $M$                                                                                                                                                                                |
| 98    | no theorems, no propositions, no error analysis (conceded by the authors)                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| 107   | four propositions: short-time total variation $\mathcal O(t)$ for the base (its square improving to $\mathcal O(t^3)$ when $\bm f$ has a bounded second derivative), transfer of expectations under total variation, KR maps tending to the identity, and the two residual blow-up rates as $t\to0$ |

One judgement runs through all four: **"a prediction should be a distribution" can be implemented at three different places in an operator-learning pipeline.** Paper 75 puts it in the encoder's confidence gate, paper 95 in the latent variable, paper 98 in the correlation structure of the prior. Paper 107 makes a different point: sometimes the right move is not to attach uncertainty to the prediction but to change the learned object to one that does not depend on the varying parameter at all.

Paper 98 adds a criterion the other three leave implicit: **the form of the uncertainty representation should be dictated by the structure of the operator.** Since $u$ is a double integral of $f$, the variance of $u$ is a global aggregation of the variance of $f$, and pointwise independent latent noise cannot express that even in principle — a question of representational capacity, not of accuracy.

There is also a shared methodological lesson in how papers 95 and 98 treat their baselines: **UQ claims are falsifiable only when the reference distribution is independent of the model being evaluated.** Paper 95 builds its reference from exact Gaussian-process posteriors pushed through a finite-difference solver, so "the deep ensemble's bands are too narrow" is a testable statement. Paper 98 has no such reference and must argue over-confidence indirectly, from the pattern of large mean errors paired with small standard deviations — a correspondingly weaker case.
